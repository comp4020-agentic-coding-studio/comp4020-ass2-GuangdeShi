# Course Voice

**Status:** Authoritative editorial guide for this course, established
during the Voice & Editorial Design stage (see
`docs/process/voice-design-transition.md`). Governs prose style only —
it does not have authority over curriculum content, chapter order,
learning outcomes, or factual claims. Where this document and
`docs/chapters/chapter-XX.md` disagree about a fact, `docs/SOURCES.md`
and the chapter files win; `VOICE.md` only ever wins on how a true thing
is said, never on what is true. See `CLAUDE.md`'s documentation
authority order, unchanged by this stage.

## Why this document exists

Assignment 2 requires more than a factually correct set of twelve
chapters. The brief specifically warns against a curriculum that reads
as "content-shaped chunks" — twelve competent, interchangeable articles
that happen to share a topic. A semester built like that would pass a
factual audit and still fail the actual assignment: it wouldn't hold
together as one idea, and it wouldn't sound like anyone in particular
wrote it.

The curriculum content produced so far (Parts I–IV, all twelve chapters,
whole-course audited) is factually complete and structurally locked.
This document defines how that content gets *said* — the voice pass that
comes after content and before presentation design.

## The voice, in one line

**A warm field notebook following one cat across a lifetime.**

The reader should feel like they are watching one real animal change
over years — not reading twelve unrelated pet-care articles that happen
to mention the same cat's name for continuity. Momo is the reason the
course holds together, not a mascot decorating separately-written
material.

## What this should sound like — and what it shouldn't

The course should sound like:

> Someone experienced is helping me notice what I would otherwise miss.

Not:

> An AI is summarising feline-care guidelines for me.

Concretely, the prose should be:

- **Concrete** — a specific carrier, a specific windowsill, a specific
  vet's exact phrase, not "provide a comfortable resting area."
- **Observant** — the kind of writing that notices a detail before it
  explains why the detail matters.
- **Warm without being sentimental** — warmth comes from accurate,
  specific observation, not from adjectives instructing the reader how
  to feel.
- **Intelligent without sounding clinical** — precise about evidence
  without adopting a guideline's register; a person can be exact and
  still sound like a person.
- **Accessible to ordinary people** — no jargon left unglossed, no
  assumption of prior cat-ownership experience, consistent with the
  broad-public-audience principle already established in `CLAUDE.md`
  §18.
- **Occasionally playful** — dry observation, mild humour about owner
  mistakes (including the writer's own hypothetical ones), never at the
  cat's expense.
- **Willing to acknowledge uncertainty** — where the evidence is
  genuinely unsettled or a source's limitation has already been
  disclosed in `docs/SOURCES.md`, the prose should let that show rather
  than smoothing it into false confidence.
- **Grounded in specific scenes and physical details** — see the chapter
  voice grammar below.
- **Respectful of the cat as an animal** — Momo is observed, not
  ventriloquised. She does not have interior monologue, dialogue, or
  human-style reasoning attributed to her. The course teaches the
  reader to read *behaviour*, which only works if the text itself
  models reading behaviour rather than inventing feelings.

## Where this voice comes from

This voice system is built from five things already established for
this project, not invented fresh for this document:

1. **The Assignment 2 requirement** for a curriculum voice distinctive
   enough that it doesn't read as generic AI-generated prose or a stack
   of interchangeable articles.
2. **Humanizer methodology** (`.claude/skills/humanizer/SKILL.md`) — for
   stripping the *shape* of AI-generated prose: formulaic structure,
   banned vocabulary, symmetrical paragraphing, hedge-padding.
3. **Storyteller methodology** (`.claude/skills/storyteller/SKILL.md`) —
   for wrapping verified evidence in scene, transition, and callback,
   selectively, never in place of the evidence.
4. **The already-approved Momo lifecycle structure** — twelve chapters
   following one cat from kitten to end-of-life, which is exactly the
   spine a field-notebook voice needs to be worth using.
5. **The broad-public-audience principle** (`CLAUDE.md` §18) and the
   **evidence-first curriculum** already completed (`docs/SOURCES.md`,
   `docs/CURRICULUM.md`, all twelve chapter files) — voice serves these,
   it doesn't compete with them.

## Not an imitation exercise

This voice does not imitate any specific living author. Broad,
long-established narrative techniques may be borrowed the way any
writer borrows craft — concrete domestic detail, observational humour,
delayed explanation, recurring objects, callbacks, contrast,
scene-before-theory, sentence-length variation, narrative continuity —
but none of these techniques belong to one writer, and the voice that
results belongs to this course, not to an imitated style.

## Anti-slop rules

These are **warning patterns**, not a mechanical ban list. Natural human
prose occasionally contains any one of them on its own. The actual tell
is when several stack together in one passage, or when a pattern repeats
identically across every chapter. Do not strip an instance just because
it appears on this list — judge whether it's doing real work in that
sentence.

**Structural tells**
- Every section following an identical cadence (claim → explanation →
  example → recap), chapter after chapter.
- Restating the heading as the first sentence beneath it.
- A closing "summary" sentence that only repeats what the paragraph
  already said.
- Symmetrical paragraph templates — same length, same internal shape,
  applied uniformly.

**Formulaic connective tissue**
- Generic opening statements that could preface any topic.
- "In this chapter, you will learn…" framing.
- Repetitive "not X, but Y" contrast constructions, used more than once
  or twice across a document.
- Fake rhetorical questions used as transitions, where the sentence
  doesn't actually need to be posed as a question.
- Motivational filler that carries no information.

**Vocabulary** (a smell test, not a ban — one instance where the word is
genuinely right is fine; several clustered together is the tell):
crucial, key, important, delve, navigate, comprehensive, holistic,
foster, enhance, robust, leverage, unlock, showcase, vibrant, harness
(as a verb), tapestry, pivotal.

**Other tells**
- Excessive em-dash use as a substitute for choosing real sentence
  structure.
- Inflated claims about the significance of a single observation or
  habit.
- Excessive bullet-point dependence where a plain sentence would read
  more naturally.
- Uniform sentence length with no short sentence ever breaking a run of
  medium ones (or the reverse).

## Chapter voice grammar

This is a flexible set of recurring editorial moves, not a mandatory
six-section template every chapter must follow in this exact order.
Some chapters will use all of these; some will use three; the order can
change; a chapter driven mainly by reasoning/method (as Ch6 and Ch9 are
already approved to be) may lean on Evidence and Transfer far more than
Scene.

- **Scene** — a concrete moment involving Momo.
- **Notice** — the detail the learner should pay attention to.
- **Question** — why that detail matters.
- **Evidence** — the relevant feline-care principle, drawn from
  `docs/SOURCES.md`, tagged DIRECT or SYNTHESIS exactly as already
  registered.
- **Return to Momo** — apply the principle back to the same animal.
- **Transfer** — show how the principle changes in another household or
  a later life stage.

## Momo must create continuity

Momo is not decoration. Later chapters should visibly remember earlier
events, using only real, already-approved curriculum dependencies — see
`.claude/skills/storyteller/SKILL.md`'s "Momo continuity" section for
the specific objects and threads already available to reuse (the
carrier, the observation log, the Chapter 8 lifestyle decision revisited
in Chapter 10, the Chapter 3 medical-first reasoning reapplied in
Chapter 11, an environment built for a kitten needing visible adaptation
by Part IV). Do not manufacture a callback where no real curriculum
dependency exists — every callback must be checkable against
`docs/CURRICULUM.md` or the relevant chapter files' own
Prerequisite/Placement sections.

## Factual immutability rule

This is the rule that matters most in this entire document.

**The editorial pass may change:** sentence structure, paragraph
structure, rhythm, examples, scene framing, transitions, explanatory
analogies, and narrative sequence where factual meaning is preserved.

**The editorial pass may NOT silently change:** claim meaning, Source
IDs, Claim IDs, DIRECT/SYNTHESIS classification, numerical thresholds,
medical escalation guidance, risk distinctions, sex-specific nuance,
source limitations, learning outcomes, prerequisite relationships, or
the approved curriculum backbone.

**If making the prose more natural would alter a factual claim: keep the
factual claim.** Voice never outranks evidence. This is not a judgement
call to be balanced against how good the sentence sounds — it is a hard
constraint, identical in spirit to `CLAUDE.md` §7's source/evidence rule
and §19's DIRECT/SYNTHESIS transparency rule, just applied at the prose
level instead of the claim-register level.

## Editorial workflow

See `CLAUDE.md`'s corresponding section for the authoritative version of
this workflow and its human-approval boundary. In summary:

```
LOCKED CONTENT
  -> VOICE PASS
  -> NARRATIVE PASS
  -> HUMANIZATION PASS
  -> EVIDENCE-PRESERVATION CHECK
  -> VOICE AUDIT
  -> APPROVED PROSE
```

Humanizer and Storyteller are editorial tools applied during this
pipeline. Neither has curriculum authority — they cannot add a claim,
remove a claim, or reinterpret a source. Ordinary prose-level
improvements (rhythm, structure, scene-framing, vocabulary) may be made
autonomously once this stage's approach is approved. A human decision is
required only if an editorial change would materially affect curriculum
meaning, factual interpretation, course thesis, chapter capability,
medical advice, or source interpretation — the same escalation
boundary already used for curriculum work, applied here to prose.
