## Teaching purpose

This chapter corrects three related misconceptions about what preventive
care actually is:

1. **"Healthy is what the annual checkup tells you."** A once-a-year
   snapshot misses a slow, gradual trend almost by definition — the same
   normalisation-drift risk taught generally in Chapter 6 (`CH6-02`),
   applied here specifically to weight and body condition rather than
   behaviour.
2. **"If she doesn't look sick, there's nothing more to check."** Weight
   and muscle condition change gradually enough that an owner living with
   a cat every day is often the *last* person to notice the change,
   precisely because it never presents as a single dramatic moment.
3. **"Researching vaccines and parasite prevention online means I can
   just tell the vet what Momo needs."** Preventive-medicine decisions
   are individualised to a specific cat's risk profile; the owner's job
   is to supply an accurate lifestyle picture, not to select the
   protocol.

**Audience note (per `CLAUDE.md` §18):** the preparation activities in
this chapter (logging weigh-ins, noting body-condition impressions
between visits) are designed to require no special equipment beyond a
notebook or phone note and a few minutes at a time — they must not
assume a household has a pet scale, unlimited time, or prior veterinary
literacy. Where a household's financial or time constraints affect *how
often* a vet visit is realistic, that constraint is treated as a real
input to the conversation the owner has with the vet, not a reason to
skip preventive care altogether.

## Concrete knowledge map

### Cluster A — Two different scales, two different questions

- **`CH7-01`** *(DIRECT)*: Body Condition Score (BCS) and Muscle
  Condition Score (MCS) are separate, independent scales. BCS assesses
  fat covering (a 9-point scale, judged by how easily ribs can be felt
  and whether a waist is visible from above; the ideal range sits in the
  middle of the scale). MCS separately assesses muscle mass (normal, or
  mild/moderate/severe muscle loss, judged over the spine, shoulders,
  and skull). A cat can be overweight (high BCS) while simultaneously
  losing muscle (abnormal MCS) — especially as she ages or if she
  becomes ill — so checking only one scale can miss a real problem the
  other would catch.
  - *Owner relevance:* Gives the student the two specific things worth
    checking between vet visits, not a vague sense of "does she look
    okay."
  - *Misconception corrected:* "If she doesn't look sick, there's
    nothing more to check" and "weight is the only thing that matters."
  - *Scope note:* The exact per-point chart wording has not been
    independently verified (the source charts are image-only); this
    course teaches the two scales' purpose and general poles, not a
    verbatim chart script. See `docs/SOURCES.md`, `S22`.

### Cluster B — Feeding as a behavioural need, not just a bowl to fill

- **`CH7-02`** *(DIRECT)*: How a cat is fed affects wellbeing as much as
  what she is fed. Foraging or puzzle feeders let a cat engage natural
  seeking behaviour rather than eating from a static bowl; small,
  frequent meals suit feline feeding biology better than one or two
  large meals a day; obesity risk is linked to an indoor, sedentary
  lifestyle; and in a multi-cat household, feeding stations should be
  visually separated from each other and kept away from litter boxes, so
  a more anxious or subordinate cat is not avoiding food access out of
  social pressure.
  - *Owner relevance:* Turns "what do I feed her" into "how do I feed
    her," which is the piece most owners never think to ask.
  - *Misconception corrected:* That feeding is a purely nutritional
    decision with no behavioural dimension.
  - *Callback:* Directly extends the "resources need space between them"
    principle already taught for litter-box placement (`CH1-03`) to
    feeding stations, and connects to Chapter 5's foraging/puzzle-feeding
    content (`CH5-02`) from a preventive-health angle rather than a
    predatory-need angle.

### Cluster C — Vaccination is a risk conversation, not a checklist

- **`CH7-03`** *(DIRECT)*: Vaccination decisions should be organised
  around a cat's individualised risk factors — life stage, environment,
  and lifestyle (indoor/outdoor access, multi-cat household, travel or
  boarding) — rather than one fixed schedule applied to every cat.
  Current guidelines explicitly move away from older, more rigid,
  one-size-fits-all vaccination protocols.
  - *Owner relevance:* Explains *why* a vet asks lifestyle questions at
    a wellness visit instead of just administering a standard set of
    shots.
  - *Misconception corrected:* That a vaccine schedule is a fixed fact
    to be looked up rather than a judgement made about a specific cat.
- **`CH7-04`** *(SYNTHESIS)*: The owner's productive role in a
  vaccination or parasite-prevention conversation is to accurately
  report the household's lifestyle risk factors (indoor/outdoor access,
  other animals in the home, travel or boarding, geographic region) to
  the vet — not to select a vaccine, choose a parasite-prevention
  product, or set a treatment schedule independently, however much
  research has gone into it.
  - *Owner relevance:* This is the chapter's explicit medical-scope
    boundary, stated as a positive instruction (what the owner *should*
    do) rather than only a prohibition.
  - *Misconception corrected:* "Researching vaccines and parasite
    prevention online means I can just tell the vet what Momo needs."
  - *Scope note:* Built from `S12`'s individualised-risk emphasis and
    `S23`'s risk-based testing cadence, neither of which is written as
    owner-facing coaching; this owner-conversation framing is this
    course's own construction, kept inside the established medical scope
    boundary (`CLAUDE.md` §21 point 8). Disclosed per `CLAUDE.md` §19.

### Cluster D — Parasite prevention follows a pattern, not a one-time check

- **`CH7-05`** *(DIRECT)*: Routine parasite prevention follows a
  testing-and-cadence pattern: a preventive exam roughly every 6–12
  months; fecal testing at least four times in a cat's first year and at
  least twice yearly for a healthy adult, more often with outdoor
  access, travel, or contact with other animals; year-round
  broad-spectrum parasite control is preferred over seasonal or
  intermittent treatment; and indoor-only status reduces, but does not
  eliminate, parasite risk.
  - *Owner relevance:* Corrects the common assumption that an
    indoor-only cat needs no parasite prevention at all.
  - *Misconception corrected:* "Indoor cats don't need parasite
    prevention" and "a single yearly test is the same thing as
    year-round prevention."
  - *Scope note:* Cadence figures are drawn from the source's default
    view; the parallel cat-specific content was not independently
    spot-checked, per `S23`'s own access-status note.

### Cluster E — Reapplying Chapter 6's method to a body, not just a behaviour

- **`CH7-06`** *(SYNTHESIS)*: Body condition, muscle condition, and
  weight belong in the same personal observation-log baseline already
  established in Chapter 1 (`CH1-09`), and should be judged using the
  same method already taught for behaviour (`CH6-01`): a single reading
  is not the concern — a gradual, unexplained trend in either direction
  is what should prompt raising it with the vet ahead of the next
  scheduled visit, rather than waiting for the annual checkup to be the
  only point of assessment.
  - *Owner relevance:* This is the chapter's central deliverable — it is
    what actually answers the core question ("what does healthy look
    like day to day") rather than leaving "day to day" as an
    unaddressed gap between visits.
  - *Misconception corrected:* "Healthy is what the annual checkup
    tells you."
  - *Callback:* Reuses `CH6-01`'s four steps directly, substituting
    "BCS/MCS/weight impression" for "behaviour" at step 2, and reuses
    `CH6-02`'s normalisation-drift warning directly — a slow physical
    change is exactly the kind of change that drift hides.
  - *Scope note:* This is this course's own extension of `CH6-01`'s
    method to a new observation category; no single source states this
    integration. Disclosed per `CLAUDE.md` §19.

## Owner-facing application

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to check Momo's
body and muscle condition between vet visits, add those observations to
the same baseline they have kept since Chapter 1, recognise a gradual
trend worth raising early rather than waiting for the next scheduled
visit, and walk into a vaccine/parasite-prevention conversation prepared
to describe Momo's actual lifestyle rather than a protocol they chose
themselves.

## Opening scenario

**What the student sees:** Momo's first annual wellness exam is three
weeks away. One housemate has been reading about "what vaccines a cat
actually needs" and wants to arrive with a specific list to request.
Another says Momo "looks totally fine" and there's nothing to prepare —
the appointment will tell them everything they need to know. Neither has
been weighing her or paying attention to how her body has felt under her
fur since she was a kitten.

**What is deliberately withheld:** Whether Momo's weight or body
condition has actually changed at all — the student does not yet have
any data to check, which is exactly the gap this chapter's method is
built to fill before the household can answer honestly.

**Decision the student must make:** What should the household actually
do in the three weeks before the appointment?

## Practice/applied activity

**Body-condition and baseline-extension activity.** The student is given
a short household log spanning several months (weight readings, brief
body-feel notes, and any household context) and must: (a) identify
whether an actual trend exists or the readings are just normal
fluctuation; (b) if a trend exists, check it against `CH7-01`'s two
scales (is it a fat-covering change, a muscle change, or both); (c) run
it through `CH7-06`/`CH6-01`'s four steps to decide whether it should be
raised with the vet now or logged and watched.

1. "Weighed monthly for four months: 4.1kg, 4.1kg, 4.2kg, 4.1kg. No
   other change noted." → Normal fluctuation, not a real trend — the
   method's correct answer is "this isn't a change to evaluate," the
   same deliberately-included noise case as Chapter 6's missed-breakfast
   example.
2. "Weighed monthly for four months: 4.0kg, 4.2kg, 4.5kg, 4.8kg. No diet
   change; ribs noticeably harder to feel than four months ago." →
   Real, gradual, unexplained upward trend in BCS → raise with the vet
   ahead of the next scheduled visit rather than waiting.
3. "Weight unchanged at 4.1kg for four months, but shoulder and spine
   feel noticeably more prominent than before." → Weight alone looks
   stable, but MCS has changed independently of BCS — exactly the case
   `CH7-01` warns a weight-only check would miss → raise with the vet.
4. "Weighed monthly: 4.0kg, 3.9kg, 4.3kg, 4.1kg — the middle reading
   coincided with two weeks of a housemate free-feeding extra treats
   during a stressful exam period, which then stopped." → An
   identifiable, benign explanation exists and the reading has already
   returned toward baseline → log the episode and keep observing,
   reusing `CH6-01`'s "explained and resolving" branch directly.
5. **A household with no pet scale, who tracks body condition only by
   feel and by comparing phone photos taken side-on and from above each
   month** — chosen to demonstrate, not just assert, this chapter's own
   Audience note (`CLAUDE.md` §18): the method does not depend on owning
   a scale. Over two months, ribs that used to be easy to feel become
   harder to find under a consistent layer, with no diet change and
   nothing else different. → A real, gradual BCS trend is still
   detectable by feel and photo comparison alone, and should still be
   raised with the vet ahead of schedule, exactly as in profile 2.

## Transfer scenario/Case Check (primary)

**Scenario.** Over three consecutive monthly weigh-ins logged in the
household's observation notebook, Momo's weight has crept up gradually,
and the housemate who handles her most has noticed her ribs are
noticeably harder to feel than they used to be. Nothing else has
changed — same food, same routine, no other symptom. Her next scheduled
wellness visit is still ten weeks away.

| Option | Text |
|---|---|
| A | Wait for the already-scheduled ten-week-out appointment, since nothing urgent is happening and the trend can just be mentioned then |
| B | Apply the baseline-extension method: recognise this as a gradual, unexplained upward BCS trend with no benign explanation, log it clearly, and contact the vet to discuss it ahead of the already-scheduled visit rather than waiting the full ten weeks |
| C | Start restricting Momo's food immediately to bring the trend back down before the appointment |
| — | **Answer: B** |

**Reasoning:** A repeats this chapter's central misconception in a new
form — treating the already-scheduled annual visit as the only valid
checkpoint is exactly the normalisation-drift risk `CH6-02` warns
against, just applied to a body-condition trend instead of a behaviour
one. C jumps straight to an owner-initiated medical intervention
(restricting food) without a vet's involvement, which oversteps the
observe → recognise → record → escalate scope this course has held
since Chapter 3 (`CH3-06`) — a trend worth escalating is worth
escalating *to the vet*, not treating unilaterally at home. B is the
only option that correctly applies `CH7-06`: the trend is real, gradual,
and unexplained (not a single noisy reading, not resolving on its own),
which is precisely the profile `CH6-01`'s method says should be raised
proactively rather than left to wait for a checkpoint that happens to
already be on the calendar.

## Secondary application case

**Pedagogical variable:** where the primary case tests recognising a
body-condition trend, this case tests the chapter's other capability —
holding the vaccination/parasite-prevention conversation correctly. It
is a distinct challenge from either failure mode in `CH6-02`, and tests
the medical-scope boundary itself rather than an observation judgement.

**Scenario.** Ahead of the same wellness visit, one housemate has spent
several evenings researching feline vaccines and parasite-prevention
products online. They arrive at the belief that Momo should get "just
the core vaccines and a monthly flea/tick product" and want to tell the
vet this directly, without discussing Momo's actual day-to-day lifestyle
(she regularly spends weekends at a housemate's parent's house, where
there is another resident cat — a detail the housemate doesn't think to
mention, since it "isn't a vaccine question" in their mind).

| Option | Text |
|---|---|
| A | Let the housemate present their researched list to the vet as the plan, since they clearly put in the effort |
| B | Before the appointment, make sure the vet hears an accurate, complete picture of Momo's actual lifestyle (any outdoor access, other animals, travel, boarding) and let the vet — not the housemate's research — decide the specific vaccines and parasite-prevention protocol from that picture |
| C | Skip the vaccine and parasite-prevention discussion entirely this year, since Momo has no symptoms and seems perfectly healthy |
| — | **Answer: B** |

**Reasoning:** A treats a well-researched owner list as equivalent to an
individualised clinical judgement — but `CH7-03` and `CH7-04` are
explicit that risk-based vaccination and parasite prevention depend on
an accurate lifestyle picture assessed by the vet, not a protocol an
owner assembles independently, however diligent the research. The
scenario is deliberately built so the housemate's own list would in fact
be wrong — it omits Momo's regular contact with another cat in a second
household entirely, which is exactly the kind of detail an
owner-selected protocol can silently miss. C
repeats Cluster A/E's misconception in a new form — treating the absence
of visible symptoms as equivalent to the absence of risk, ignoring that
vaccination and parasite prevention are preventive by design, not a
response to existing illness. B is the only option that keeps the owner
in their correct role: supplying complete, accurate information, and
leaving the specific medical decision to the vet.

## Common misconceptions

1. Healthy is whatever the annual checkup finds — there's nothing
   meaningful to check in between visits.
2. If a cat doesn't look sick, there's nothing more to monitor.
3. Weight is the only thing worth tracking for body condition; muscle
   condition doesn't need a separate check.
4. Researching vaccines or parasite-prevention products online qualifies
   an owner to choose the specific protocol themselves.
5. An indoor-only cat has no meaningful parasite risk and doesn't need
   routine prevention.

## Placement justification

**Why here:** This chapter must come first in Part III because it is
the direct extension of Part II's capstone method (`CH6-01`) into a new
observation category, and because it establishes the body/muscle-
condition baseline-extension habit that Chapter 9 directly reuses
(`CH9-06`) and Chapter 10 reapplies again in a new form (`CH10-04`).

**Progression-swap test:** If this chapter were moved before Chapter 6,
`CH7-06` would have no method to extend — it exists specifically to
reapply `CH6-01`, which must already be taught. If moved after Chapter 8
or Chapter 9, Part III would open on a lifestyle-risk or subtle-signs
chapter without the baseline-extension groundwork those chapters lean
on, and the course would have already asked the student to reason about
harder, subtler physical signs before teaching the basic body/muscle
vocabulary this chapter provides.

**What is lost if removed:** Without this chapter, the course would move
directly from a behavioural baseline-comparison method (Chapter 6) to a
major lifestyle trade-off (Chapter 8) without ever giving the student a
concrete, checkable vocabulary for physical health status — leaving
"keep her well" as an abstract goal rather than something the student
can actually assess between vet visits, and removing the course's only
explicit treatment of the owner's correct role in a vaccination/parasite-
prevention conversation.
