## Teaching purpose

This chapter corrects two misconceptions, both about *how* to decide
something has changed, not about any single behaviour:

1. **"I'll know it when I see it."** Without a baseline and a method, an
   owner's sense of "something's off" is unreliable — too slow for a
   change that matters, too jumpy for one that doesn't. Chapters 1–5
   already gave the student pieces of a method (a log, escalation
   signatures, combined-signal reading, need-vs-medical triage); this
   chapter is where those pieces are assembled into one procedure the
   student can actually run.
2. **"If I can explain why she's behaving differently, it's fine."** An
   explanation for a change (a houseguest, a schedule disruption) makes a
   change *less alarming*, but only if the change actually resolves once
   the explanation passes. An explanation is not automatically a
   dismissal.

## Prerequisite capability

This chapter is the direct integration point for four earlier

- **Chapter 1** (`CH1-09`): the daily observation log is the baseline
  this entire chapter's method compares against. Without it, "what
  changed" has no reference point.
- **Chapter 3** (`CH3-02`, `CH3-03`, `CH3-06`): already taught that
  behaviours can have distinct causes requiring different responses, and
  gave the student one concrete escalation signature to recognise
  on sight.
- **Chapter 4** (`CH4-04`): combined-signal reading is what lets the
  student notice a change accurately in the first place, rather than
  react to a single misread cue.
- **Chapter 5** (`CH5-04`): the environmental/behavioural/medical triage
  categories are reused directly here as the shape of the decision this
  chapter's method produces.

## Concrete knowledge map

### Cluster A — Why "I'll know it when I see it" fails

- No new claim here; this cluster motivates the method using worked
  recollection of Chapters 1–5 rather than new sourced content. It makes
  explicit that a baseline (`CH1-09`) is what turns a vague feeling into
  a comparison. It opens with a short **recall worksheet**: before
  Beat 5 formalises `CH6-01`, the student is asked to write down, from
  memory, the specific rule each of four earlier chapters contributed —
  Chapter 1's daily log, Chapter 3's obstruction-risk signature
  (`CH3-06`), Chapter 4's combined-signal reading (`CH4-04`), and
  Chapter 5's need-vs-medical triage (`CH5-04`) — then checks their
  answers against a short reference panel before Beat 5 shows how the
  four pieces click together into one method.
  - *Owner relevance:* Names the exact gap the rest of the chapter fills.
  - *Misconception corrected:* "I'll know it when I see it."

### Cluster B — The baseline-comparison method

- **`CH6-01`** *(SYNTHESIS)*: A four-step method — (1) recall the
  personal baseline; (2) identify specifically what changed (frequency,
  intensity, duration, a new behaviour, or an absent usual one); (3)
  check whether the change matches a known urgent-escalation signature —
  if so, escalate immediately regardless of any apparent explanation;
  (4) if not urgent, ask whether an identifiable, benign cause accounts
  for it — if so, and the change resolves once the cause passes, log and
  keep observing; if no explanation exists, or the change persists or
  worsens, raise it with the vet rather than continuing to wait.
  - *Owner relevance:* This is the chapter's central deliverable — a
    procedure the student can run on any behaviour, not just the ones
    already taught.
  - *Misconception corrected:* Both "I'll know it when I see it" and "an
    explanation always means it's fine."
  - *Callback:* Step 3 reuses `CH3-06` directly; step 4's "not urgent but
    unexplained → vet conversation" tier reuses `CH5-04`'s
    medical-first reasoning; the whole method depends on `CH1-09`'s log
    and `CH4-04`'s reading skill to even reach step 2 accurately.
  - *Scope note:* This is a course-constructed synthesis, disclosed per
    `CLAUDE.md` §19 — no single guideline states this exact procedure.

### Cluster C — The two failure modes

- **`CH6-02`** *(SYNTHESIS)*: Normalisation drift (explaining away a real,
  gradual change because the baseline itself quietly shifted with the
  cat) and over-escalation (treating every ordinary variation as alarm-
  worthy) are symmetrical failure modes. Neither permanent "wait and
  see" nor permanent "escalate everything" is the correct application of
  `CH6-01`'s method.
  - *Owner relevance:* Names the two ways owners get this wrong in
    opposite directions — under-reacting and over-reacting are both
    method failures, not two different problems.
  - *Misconception corrected:* That being "extra careful" (escalating
    everything) is a safe default with no real cost.
  - *Scope note:* A pedagogical caution rather than a guideline claim;
    exists specifically to keep this method realistically ambiguous
    rather than a mechanical checklist with one obviously right answer
    every time.

## Owner-facing application

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to take any
observed change in Momo's behaviour — including ones never explicitly
taught in this course — and run the same four-step method to decide
whether it needs a vet conversation, ongoing observation, or nothing at
all, rather than relying on instinct or an explanation alone.

## Opening scenario

**What the student sees:** For the past several days, Momo has been
eating noticeably less at her morning meal and has been more vocal in
the evenings. One housemate says "she's just going through a phase,
cats are like that sometimes." Another says it's probably nothing since
she's still using the litter box normally and seems otherwise energetic.

**What is deliberately withheld:** Whether anything in the household has
changed recently (a new food, a schedule shift, a houseguest) — the
student does not yet know whether an explanation exists, which is
exactly the missing piece the method is built to surface.

**Decision the student must make:** What should happen next, given only
what's observable so far?

## Opening question

| Option | Text | Intended? | Why tempting | Misconception revealed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Wait and see indefinitely — she's still eating something and using the litter box normally, so there's no urgent sign | No | Matches the earlier, correct "wait and observe" default from Chapter 1/3 for a genuinely mild case — but applied here without first checking whether an explanation exists or the change is resolving | Treats "not an emergency" as equivalent to "no action needed," skipping steps 2 and 4 of the method entirely |
| B | Apply the baseline-comparison method: check exactly what changed against the log, rule out an urgent signature, then look for an identifiable explanation before deciding whether to wait or raise it with the vet | **Yes** | Requires checking the log and asking a question rather than reacting on instinct | None — this is the method the chapter is built to install |
| C | Treat any deviation from her normal routine as a reason for an immediate vet visit, just to be safe | No | Feels maximally cautious and risk-averse | Over-escalation (`CH6-02`) — ignores that most day-to-day variation is not urgent, and that treating everything as an emergency is unsustainable and erodes the owner's ability to notice a change that actually matters |

## Practice/applied activity

**Method walk-through activity.** The student is given four short
change-reports (each just a few lines, in the voice of the daily log)
and must run all four steps of `CH6-01` explicitly, writing down: (a)
what specifically changed, (b) whether it matches an urgent signature,
(c) whether an explanation exists, and (d) the resulting decision
(escalate now / raise with vet, not urgent / log and keep observing).

1. "She's been crying loudly every time she tries to go to the litter
   box today, and hasn't produced much." → Matches `CH3-06`'s
   obstruction-risk signature → escalate now, regardless of any
   explanation.
2. "She's been sleeping more than usual this week — we also just had
   the heating fixed and the apartment's noticeably warmer now." → No
   urgent signature; explanation exists (warmer environment);
   decision depends on whether the change resolves as temperature
   stabilises — log and keep observing for now.
3. "She's been more withdrawn for about ten days. Nothing's changed
   that we can think of." → No urgent signature; no explanation found →
   raise with the vet rather than continuing to wait indefinitely.
4. "She missed one breakfast because we forgot to feed her on time,
   then ate normally at lunch." → Not a pattern at all — a single,
   fully explained, one-off event; the method's answer is "this isn't
   really a change to evaluate," which is itself a correct application
   (deliberately included so the student doesn't over-apply the method
   to noise).

## Transfer scenario/Case Check (primary)

**Scenario.** A housemate's cousin stays for two weeks. During the
visit, Momo's scratching becomes noticeably more frequent and intense,
concentrated on the same doorframe near the guest room. In the week
after the guest leaves, the scratching returns to its previous baseline
level without any other intervention.

| Option | Text |
|---|---|
| A | Since the scratching has already returned to normal, no further action or log entry is needed — the case is closed |
| B | Log the full episode (what changed, the plausible explanation, and that it resolved once the explanation passed) as a confirmed example of an explained, resolved change — useful evidence for judging a similar future episode, even though no vet conversation was needed this time |
| C | Because the scratching became more intense during the episode, treat it as a new baseline going forward, regardless of the fact that it returned to the earlier level afterward |
| — | **Answer: B** |

**Reasoning:** A is close, but misses that `CH6-01`'s method is only
useful long-term if outcomes are actually logged — an episode that
resolves without a vet visit still updates the owner's confidence in the
method and their baseline data, which matters the next time a change
looks similar but does *not* resolve on its own. C repeats
`CH6-02`'s normalisation-drift failure in reverse: treating a temporary,
explained spike as the new normal would make a real future change harder
to detect, not easier. B is the only option that both correctly
identifies this as a resolved, explained case (no escalation needed) and
recognises that the method's value depends on the baseline being
maintained accurately across episodes, not just applied once.

## Secondary application case

**Pedagogical variable:** where the primary case tests judging an
explained, resolving change correctly, this case tests applying the
method when the baseline data itself is incomplete — a distinct
challenge from either failure mode in `CH6-02`, and one that echoes
Chapter 4's secondary case (information availability when firsthand
observation isn't possible) without repeating it.

**Scenario.** Two housemates share care of Momo. One keeps the daily
observation log consistently; the other does not log anything and
relies on memory. They disagree about whether Momo's appetite has
actually decreased over the past week, or whether it "seems that way"
because neither of them has been paying close attention.

| Option | Text |
|---|---|
| A | Trust whichever housemate feels more confident, since a confident impression is usually reliable |
| B | Recognise that the disagreement itself is a signal the baseline is incomplete, and address that first — compare what log entries do exist, reconstruct the missing days as accurately as possible together, and only then apply the four-step method with appropriately lower confidence in step 2 |
| C | Since neither housemate has complete data, conclude the method can't be used at all and default to "wait and see" |
| — | **Answer: B** |

**Reasoning:** A repeats a version of `CH4-04`'s single-cue-confidence
error — confidence is not the same as accuracy, whether the "cue" is a
meow or a housemate's impression. C over-corrects: incomplete data
weakens step 2's precision, but the method doesn't stop working with
imperfect data, it just needs to account for that imperfection
explicitly rather than being abandoned. B is the only option that
treats incomplete logging as a solvable problem (reconstruct what can be
reconstructed, as in Chapter 4's night-shift case) rather than either
trusting an unverified impression or giving up on the method entirely.

## Common misconceptions

1. A behaviour change is only worth acting on if you can immediately
   sense that "something's off," without needing to check it against
   anything.
2. If a change can be explained, it can be dismissed without needing to
   confirm it actually resolves.
3. Being maximally cautious (treating every variation as urgent) is a
   safe default with no downside.
4. A single explained, one-off event (like a missed feeding time) is the
   same kind of thing as a sustained behaviour change, and needs the
   same method applied to it.
5. If the observation log is incomplete, the whole method becomes
   useless and there's no point trying to apply it.

## Placement justification

**Why here:** This chapter is the Part II capstone and must come last in
the part — it cannot be taught before Chapters 1, 3, 4, and 5 exist to
integrate, since its entire content is the explicit assembly of those
chapters' separate pieces into one method.

**Progression-swap test:** If this chapter were moved earlier in Part
II, it would have nothing yet to integrate — `CH6-01`'s steps 3 and 4
directly reuse `CH3-06` and `CH5-04`, which must already be taught. If
moved into Part III, Chapter 7 would lose its dependency (assessing
"healthy day to day" requires already being able to judge whether
day-to-day *is* healthy, which is this chapter's method) and Part II
would end on Chapter 5's specific-behaviour content without ever
generalising it into a transferable skill.

**What is lost if removed:** Without this chapter, the course would
teach five chapters of increasingly sophisticated observation and
response without ever explicitly naming or practising the general
method that connects them — leaving the student able to handle the
specific cases already taught, but not equipped to reason about a novel
change on their own, which is precisely the "interpreter" stage of the
course's owner-progression arc (`docs/CURRICULUM.md`, Course
progression).
