"just getting old" explains reduced activity without checking for joint

principle concrete rather than abstract.

## Teaching purpose

This chapter corrects two misconceptions:

1. **"She's just losing her mind in her old age."** Nighttime
   vocalising, house-soiling, disorientation, and withdrawal from play
   are genuinely associated with feline cognitive dysfunction — but they
   are also signs of arthritis, kidney disease, hyperthyroidism,
   hypertension, and periodontal disease, several of which this course
   has already taught the student to recognise separately (Chapters 3, 9,
   and 10). Concluding "cognitive decline" without a full veterinary
   workup repeats Chapter 3's original medical-first error in a new,
   higher-stakes context — a senior cat.
2. **"If I can match her behaviour to a cognitive-dysfunction symptom
   list, I've effectively diagnosed her."** A rule-out diagnosis is a
   clinical process (bloodwork, exam, sometimes imaging) that eliminates
   several other named conditions first — it is not something a symptom
   checklist alone can do, no matter how well the behaviour matches.

**Audience note (`CLAUDE.md` §18).** Households vary in how quickly a
change in an aging cat's behaviour gets attributed to "she's just old"
versus investigated — this often tracks how much a household already
distrusts vague age-based explanations from earlier chapters, how many
competing demands are on their attention right now, and whether more than
one household member observes the cat regularly. The practice activity
varies this directly rather than treating every household as equally
quick (or slow) to notice and question a change.

## Concrete knowledge map

### Cluster A — What cognitive dysfunction can look like

- **`CH11-01`** *(DIRECT, S25)*: Signs associated with feline cognitive
  dysfunction, most clearly noticeable from roughly age 10 onward,
  include spatial disorientation, wandering into unfamiliar territory,
  lost interest in play, excessive sleeping, altered sleep/wake cycles
  (including nighttime restlessness), long blank-staring episodes,
  indifference to food/water, house-soiling, and unprompted loud
  vocalising, often at night.
  - *Owner relevance:* Gives the student a concrete sign list to notice
    and log, not a vague sense that "something's different."
  - *Misconception corrected:* Sets up, but does not by itself resolve,
    "she's just losing her mind" — this cluster names the signs; Cluster
    C is what prevents the student from stopping here.

### Cluster B — Why this can't be diagnosed from a checklist

- **`CH11-02`** *(DIRECT, S25)*: A cognitive-dysfunction diagnosis is
  explicitly a rule-out process — arthritis, kidney failure,
  hyperthyroidism, hypertension, and periodontal disease must be excluded
  first, sometimes requiring imaging or specialist evaluation for
  neurological causes; any medication is something a vet may prescribe
  only after that rule-out process, never something an owner starts
  independently based on a symptom match.
  - *Owner relevance:* Makes the diagnostic process's difficulty
    concrete, rather than asserting "see a vet" as an unexplained rule.
  - *Misconception corrected:* "If I can match her behaviour to a
    cognitive-dysfunction symptom list, I've effectively diagnosed her."

### Cluster C — The Chapter 3 callback, made concrete

- **`CH11-03`** *(SYNTHESIS)*: Several of Cluster A's signs are the *same
  observable behaviours* this course has already taught as symptoms of
  other, separately treatable conditions — nighttime vocalising and
  house-soiling were taught in Chapter 3 as possible urinary-tract signs;
  reduced interest in play and withdrawal were taught in Chapter 10 as
  possible joint-pain signs. Reapplying `CH3-03`'s principle here means
  the student's job is to report the full, specific behavioural picture
  to a vet — not to privately settle on "cognitive decline" because it
  is the explanation that occurred to them first.
  - *Owner relevance:* This is the chapter's central teaching move —
    makes the abstract "never assume a cause away" principle concrete by
    showing the student signs they were already taught to attribute
    elsewhere.
  - *Misconception corrected:* "She's just losing her mind in her old
    age," directly, by showing the same signs have credible alternative
    explanations this course already taught.
  - *Callback:* Direct, deliberate reapplication of `CH3-03`/`CH3-06`, as
    requested at the human decision gate; also references `CH9-04`
    (dental signs) and `CH10-01` (joint pain) as concrete alternative
    explanations.
  - *Scope note:* Course-constructed integration, disclosed per
    `CLAUDE.md` §19 — no single source states this specific cross-chapter
    overlap.

### Cluster D — Owner-support suggestions, scaled to budgets of care

- **`CH11-04`** *(SYNTHESIS, S25+S15)*: Suggestions such as
  avoiding introducing a new animal into the household, using low-sided
  and easily accessible litter boxes, and adding ramps to frequently used
  surfaces are worth discussing with a vet (including whether a
  specific diet is appropriate for this cat) — but which of these a
  household actually implements should be scaled to that household's own
  budgets of care (`CH10-02`), exactly as already taught for physical
  aging changes, not treated as a uniform list every caregiver must fully
  adopt.
  - *Owner relevance:* Prevents the student from treating a second
    symptom-and-suggestion list (this chapter's) any differently from
    the first (Chapter 10's) just because the underlying cause is
    cognitive rather than physical.
  - *Misconception corrected:* Reinforces Chapter 10's correction against
    treating any senior-care list as a uniform, unscaled standard.
  - *Scope note:* `S25` states the suggestion list; the explicit
    scaling-to-budgets frame is this course's own reapplication of
    `CH10-02`. Disclosed per `CLAUDE.md` §19.

### Cluster E — The two failure modes, reapplied

- **`CH11-05`** *(SYNTHESIS)*: Chapter 6's two failure modes reappear
  here in a cognitive-specific form: normalisation drift is dismissing a
  real, gradual behavioural change as "she's just old" indefinitely
  without ever raising it; over-escalation here specifically means
  concluding cognitive dysfunction and requesting medication from a vet
  without having actually completed — or without a vet having actually
  completed — the rule-out process `CH11-02` describes.
  - *Owner relevance:* Names the two symmetrical ways an owner can get
    this specific situation wrong, matching the course's established
    pattern from Chapter 6.
  - *Misconception corrected:* Both this chapter's misconceptions, stated
    as a single symmetrical pair rather than two unrelated errors.
  - *Callback:* Direct structural reapplication of `CH6-02`.
  - *Scope note:* Course-constructed integration, disclosed per
    `CLAUDE.md` §19.

## Owner-facing application

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to notice
behavioural signs commonly associated with feline cognitive dysfunction,
recognise that several of those same signs have credible alternative
explanations already taught elsewhere in this course, report the full
specific pattern to a vet rather than privately concluding a diagnosis,
and — once a vet has actually completed the rule-out process — scale any
resulting owner-support suggestions to their household's real budgets of
care.

## Opening scenario

**What the student sees:** A few years after Chapter 10, Momo has begun
yowling loudly most nights, seems to stare blankly at walls sometimes,
and has had two house-soiling accidents this month despite a clean,
accessible litter box. One housemate says, "she's just losing her mind a
bit, that happens to old cats" and starts looking up cognitive-dysfunction
supplements online.

**What is deliberately withheld:** Whether anything else has changed —
appetite, thirst, mobility, dental signs — because the student does not
yet know whether the household has actually looked, or has jumped
straight to a conclusion from the vocalising and staring alone.

**Decision the student must make:** What should happen next, given only
the housemate's "she's just losing her mind" framing and an online
supplement search already underway?

## Practice/applied activity

**Sign-attribution activity.** The student is given four short household
scenarios, each varying how quickly and thoroughly the household notices
and questions a behaviour change (per `CLAUDE.md` §18), and must decide
what to do next and why:

1. **A single, very attentive owner who keeps a detailed log and notices
   a new sign almost immediately.** Nighttime yowling begins; the owner's
   log already shows no dental, mobility, or appetite changes present. →
   Even with a fast, thorough log, the answer is still to bring the full
   (negative) picture to a vet rather than concluding cognitive
   dysfunction alone from one sign (`CH11-02`, `CH11-03`) — thoroughness
   changes how fast the pattern is caught, not what conclusion the
   household is entitled to draw from it.
2. **A busy household with three housemates, none of whom log
   consistently, who only compare notes about once a month.** Nighttime
   yowling and one house-soiling accident have each been separately
   noticed by different housemates but never connected until this
   month's check-in. → The disconnected observations, once assembled,
   still call for the same next step — raise the assembled pattern with
   the vet — but this profile also demonstrates why `CH9-06`'s and
   `CH1-09`'s shared-log habit matters especially in a multi-person
   household (`CH11-01`, `CH11-03`).
3. **A household already fatigued by Chapter 10's aging-care routine,
   inclined to attribute any new sign to "she's just old now" as a
   low-effort default.** New nighttime restlessness appears. → This is
   exactly `CH11-05`'s normalisation-drift risk in its most likely real
   form — a household that has correctly done a lot of senior-care work
   already but risks under-reacting to something new precisely because
   they're tired of raising things (`CH11-05`).
4. **A household eager to "do everything right" who, on noticing one
   staring episode, immediately searches for and orders a
   cognitive-support supplement before their next scheduled vet visit.**
   → This is `CH11-05`'s over-escalation risk — good intentions,
   wrong order of operations; the supplement question belongs in the vet
   conversation after the rule-out process, not before it (`CH11-02`,
   `CH11-05`).

## Transfer scenario/Case Check (primary)

**Scenario.** Momo has begun wandering toward unfamiliar corners of the
apartment at night and sleeping at different times than usual. This
month, there has been one house-soiling accident. A careful household
check finds no dental warning signs (per Chapter 9's list) and no
mobility changes (per Chapter 10's signs) — appetite and thirst are also
unchanged.

| Option | Text |
|---|---|
| A | Since dental and mobility checks came back clear, conclude this is cognitive dysfunction and start looking into supportive supplements right away |
| B | Bring the full pattern — disorientation, altered sleep/wake cycle, the house-soiling accident, and the fact that dental and mobility checks were clear — to a vet, since a rule-out diagnosis (including kidney, thyroid, and blood pressure) requires clinical testing this household cannot do at home |
| C | Since nothing looks urgent by Chapter 3's emergency-signature standard, there's no need to raise it with the vet at all right now |
| — | **Answer: B** |
| |

**Reasoning:** A treats "ruled out the two things I could check myself"
as equivalent to "ruled in cognitive dysfunction" — but `CH11-02`'s
rule-out list includes conditions (kidney failure, hyperthyroidism,
hypertension) that require bloodwork or blood pressure measurement, not a
household observation check. C correctly recognises this isn't an
emergency by Chapter 3's standard, but wrongly concludes that means no
action is needed at all — a persisting, multi-sign pattern with no benign
explanation still warrants a vet conversation per `CH6-01`, just not an
emergency one. B is the only option that reports the complete picture,
including the negative findings, and defers the actual rule-out process
to where it belongs.

## Secondary application case

**Pedagogical variable:** where the primary case tests a household that
has already ruled out other explanations correctly and still needs to
avoid self-diagnosing, this case tests the callback in its most direct
form — a household that skips the check Cluster C exists to prompt, and
the alternative explanation turns out to be the correct one.

**Scenario.** A cat has begun yowling at night and eating less. A
housemate says, "she's just losing her mind, cats get like this when
they're old" and starts researching cognitive-support diets. Another
housemate notices, almost in passing, that the cat's breath has smelled
noticeably worse for a couple of weeks — a sign from Chapter 9's dental
warning-sign list — but nobody has connected it to the yowling and
appetite change.

| Option | Text |
|---|---|
| A | Continue researching cognitive-support diets, since the yowling and appetite loss are consistent with cognitive dysfunction regardless of the breath change |
| B | Recognise the noticed breath change as a Chapter 9 dental warning sign that could plausibly explain both the reduced appetite (painful eating) and the yowling (a pain vocalisation), and raise the *combined* pattern — not just the cognitive-looking signs — with the vet |
| C | Assume the breath change and the yowling/appetite loss are two unrelated issues happening at the same time by coincidence, and address them as separate problems on separate timelines |
| — | **Answer: B** |

**Reasoning:** A is exactly the error `CH11-03` exists to prevent —
concluding cognitive dysfunction while ignoring a concrete alternative
explanation the household itself already noticed. C avoids the
cognitive-decline assumption but introduces a new error — treating two
signs that plausibly share one underlying cause as unrelated, which would
lead the household to raise a less complete, less useful picture with the
vet. B is the only option that connects the dental sign to the other
symptoms as a single plausible explanation and reports the whole pattern
together, exactly as `CH11-03` is built to teach.

## Common misconceptions

1. Nighttime vocalising, house-soiling, or disorientation in a senior cat
   automatically means cognitive dysfunction.
2. Matching a cat's behaviour to a cognitive-dysfunction symptom list is
   equivalent to a diagnosis.
3. Signs that could indicate cognitive dysfunction can't also have a
   different, already-taught explanation (dental pain, joint pain, a
   urinary issue) at the same time.
4. Once dental and mobility signs are checked and found clear, no further
   medical rule-out is needed before concluding cognitive dysfunction.
5. Ordering a cognitive-support supplement or diet based on a symptom
   match is a reasonable substitute for, or precursor to, a vet
   conversation rather than something to discuss after one.
6. A household that is already doing extensive senior-care work (per
   Chapter 10) is unlikely to under-react to a new sign, since they're
   already engaged and attentive.

## Placement justification

**Why here:** This chapter must follow Chapter 10 (it reuses the budgets-
of-care frame directly) and Chapter 9 (its callback depends on the
dental sign list already being taught), and must precede Chapter 12 (the
capstone depends on both aging chapters' reasoning). It could not be
placed in Part II or III, since its central content — reapplying medical-
first reasoning to a senior cat's cognitive signs — has no meaning before
Momo is old enough for cognitive dysfunction to be a plausible
explanation at all.

**Progression-swap test:** If this chapter were moved before Chapter 10,
it would lose its most concrete callback material (`CH10-01`'s joint-pain
signs, `CH10-02`'s budgets frame) and the "you've seen these signs before"
teaching move in Cluster C would have half as much to point back to. If
moved after Chapter 12, the capstone would lose one of its two
foundational inputs.

**What is lost if removed:** Without this chapter, the human decision
gate's explicit request for a deliberate Chapter 3 reasoning callback in
Part IV would go unmet, and the course would lose its clearest
demonstration that "never assume a cause away" is a durable, transferable
principle rather than a rule specific to kittenhood house-soiling.
