## Teaching purpose

Chapter 1 taught one blunt rule: don't force interaction. That rule gets a
new owner through arrival day, but it doesn't teach what to do once Momo
*is* approaching, playing, and inviting contact — which starts happening
within days. Three linked misconceptions are addressed here:

1. That "she came to me, so she's consenting to whatever happens next" —
   treating initiation as a permanent green light rather than a
   moment-by-moment signal that must be re-read as the interaction
   continues.
2. That the sensitive window for shaping a kitten's comfort with people is
   either irrelevant ("she'll turn out how she turns out") or already
   closed and therefore not worth thinking about — rather than understood
   as closing but not yet closed, which changes how deliberately the
   next few weeks should be handled.
3. That play biting/scratching is "just what kittens do" and will resolve
   on its own, rather than a handling pattern that gets set now, for better
   or worse, by what it is paired with.

**Audience note (`CLAUDE.md` §18):** this chapter's transfer case and
secondary case deliberately use two different supervising adults — a
grandparent and a visiting child's parent — rather than one idealised
visitor. The two cases are not there for demographic variety on its own;
each surfaces a genuinely different pedagogical variable (see Transfer
scenario / Case Check and Secondary application case below).

## Prerequisite capability

Chapter 1: the student already treats "observe before acting" and "the cat
sets the pace" as baseline literacy, and has a running daily log. Chapter 2
does not repeat "don't force interaction" as a rule — it upgrades it into a
skill: recognising *when* interaction is currently welcome, not just
whether to attempt it at all.

## Concrete knowledge map

### Cluster A — Reading her signals, continuously

- **Positive/engaging signals**: ears forward, tail up, rubbing against a
  hand or person, moving closer, relaxed posture (`CH2-05`).
- **Protective/negative signals**, in three families: inhibition (freezing,
  half-shut eyes, ears rotated back — easy to miss because it looks calm),
  avoidance (moving or fleeing away), and repelling (growling, hissing,
  swatting, biting) (`CH2-05`). *Misconception corrected:* the "quiet,
  still" cat is often assumed relaxed, when inhibition can look similar to
  contentment but means the opposite.
- **A consent-check, not a one-time decision**: offer a hand, pause, and
  read her response before continuing; keep re-checking as the interaction
  continues, because her state can change mid-interaction (`CH2-06`).
  *Owner relevance:* this converts "don't force interaction" from a rule
  about *whether* to interact into a skill for *how* to interact,
  continuously.
- **Rapport isn't transferable**: a person who hasn't built their own
  relationship with her doesn't inherit someone else's (`CH2-06`).

### Cluster B — The window that's closing, not closed

- **The sensitive period runs roughly 2–3 weeks to 9–10 weeks of age**
  (`CH2-01`); Momo, adopted around 10 weeks, is at or just past its classic
  closing edge (`CH2-03`, `SYNTHESIS`). *Misconception corrected:* this is
  not "too late, so it doesn't matter" — it is "later, so it matters more
  to get right."
- **The experience asymmetry**: one bad early experience can outweigh many
  good ones (`CH2-02`). *Owner relevance:* raises the cost of a careless
  introduction (e.g. an overexcited child grabbing her) precisely during
  the weeks this chapter covers.
- **What this does *not* mean**: it is not a claim that Momo's personality
  is now fixed, or that later positive experiences don't count — only that
  this particular window is unusually efficient, and closing.

### Cluster C — Letting her lead new introductions

- **New people, animals, and stimuli should be introduced on her terms**:
  let her approach and engage first; pair new experiences with food or
  other rewards; avoid a bad first exposure (`CH2-04`).

### Cluster D — Handling with a purpose: play and cooperative care

- **Redirect play onto toys, not hands or feet** — a bite that's harmless
  from a 10-week-old kitten's mouth sets a pattern that isn't harmless
  later (`CH2-07`).
- **Carrier and handling training should be gradual and reward-based**, not
  something that only appears (and is only ever forced) right before a vet
  visit — building directly on Chapter 1's "carrier as a permanent, familiar
  object" (`CH2-08`).

## Owner-facing application

By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to: notice the
difference between a cat leaning into a hand and one merely tolerating it;
stop an interaction at the first sign of inhibition or avoidance rather
than only at active repelling; redirect a play-bite to a toy in the moment;
explain to a well-meaning visitor or child, in plain terms, how to let the
kitten approach rather than reach for her; and start one calm,
reward-paired carrier practice session.

## Opening scenario

**Status: PROPOSED.**

It's been a few days. Momo, more confident now, approaches the student
sitting on the floor and headbutts their hand. The student begins petting
her. After a few strokes, her tail starts to flick and her ears rotate back
slightly — small, easy-to-miss changes, not obvious aggression.

What the student sees: a cat who *approached first* and now shows two
small, ambiguous signals partway through.

What is deliberately withheld: whether these signals will escalate to
something obvious (they might not) — the point is to decide from the small
signal, not wait for a big one.

Decision the student must make: continue petting, stop, or pick her up.

## Opening question

**Status: PROPOSED.**

**Question:** Momo's tail starts to flick and her ears turn back while you're petting her. What do you do?

| Option | Text | Intended? | Why tempting | Misconception revealed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Keep petting — she came over herself, so she clearly wants attention | No | "She initiated this" feels like durable consent | Treats a single starting signal as a standing permission, ignoring that her state can change mid-interaction |
| B | Stop petting and let her decide what happens next | **Yes** | — | — |
| C | Pick her up to settle her down, since she seems a little unsettled | No | Feels like comforting/caretaking | Escalates contact exactly when the signal calls for reducing it — the same handling error as forcing interaction, just gentler-looking |

## Practice / applied activity

**Status: PROPOSED (description only — no UI/interaction implementation at
this stage).** The student reviews four short described interactions (not
video — text/still-image vignettes) and, for each, identifies which family
of signal (positive, inhibition, avoidance, repelling) is present and what
the correct next action is. At least one vignette should show a signal
changing partway through, mirroring the opening scenario, to test the
"continuous consent-check" idea rather than a single static read.

## Transfer scenario / Case Check

**Status: PROPOSED draft.**

**Scenario:** Momo's new owner's grandmother is visiting this weekend and,
delighted to meet the kitten, wants to pick her up and hold her for a
while.

**Options:**
- A. Let grandma pick Momo up right away — it's a special occasion and grandma will be gentle.
- B. Ask grandma to sit down, let Momo approach in her own time, and only let grandma pet or hold her if Momo shows clear positive signals — stopping if Momo shows inhibition or avoidance.
- C. Keep Momo in a separate room for the whole visit to avoid any risk.

**Answer:** B.

**Reasoning:** Distinct from Chapter 1's transfer case (which asked *whether*
visitors should come at all, on arrival day). This asks a different
question: *given* that a specific, well-meaning person wants contact, how
should it be conducted? A repeats the "well-meaning but forcing" error from
Chapter 1 in a new guise (age of the person, not urgency, is now the
tempting justification); C repeats the "space means total isolation"
over-correction. B is the only option that applies this chapter's actual
new content — reading her signals in the moment — rather than just
repeating Chapter 1's macro-level answer.

**Misconception tested:** That good intentions (grandma will be gentle) or
caution (keep her away entirely) substitute for actually reading the cat's
signals during the interaction itself.

## Secondary application case

**Status: PROPOSED draft.** Per `CLAUDE.md` §18, this chapter pairs the
primary transfer case above with a second, deliberately different real
household situation — not for variety's own sake, but because it surfaces a
different variable: who is actually responsible for reading the signals and
enforcing a stop.

**Scenario:** A friend's child, visiting with a parent, is excited to meet
Momo and moves toward her quickly, talking loudly, hands already reaching.

**Options:**
- A. Let the child pick Momo up right away — kids and kittens are meant to be together.
- B. Have the supervising parent set one concrete rule before contact ("if Momo moves away, we stop — we don't follow her"), and have the child offer a hand or a toy slowly rather than reaching to grab, with the parent watching and ready to redirect.
- C. Keep Momo away from the child for the whole visit, since children and cats shouldn't mix without extensive training.

**Answer:** B.

**Reasoning:** This is not a claim that a child is inherently less capable of
reading cat signals than an adult — that would be exactly the kind of
unsupported age-related assumption `CLAUDE.md` §18 rules out. The actual
variable is **who is positioned to enforce the rule**: in the grandma case
(beat 9), the adult interacting with Momo is also the one who can be asked
to read her signals and stop. Here, the person interacting (the child) is
not the one who can be relied on to notice and respect a subtle withdrawal
signal in the moment, so the supervising adult must set and enforce a
simple, concrete rule *before* contact starts, rather than trying to teach
real-time signal-reading judgement to the child on the spot. This also
differs from the grandma case in the specific variables named in `CLAUDE.md`
§18 — movement speed and noise (fast, loud approach vs. a calm adult),
supervision structure (a third party enforcing the rule vs. self-supervised
adult), and predictability of interaction. A repeats the forcing error in a
new guise; C repeats the "space means total isolation" over-correction,
and risks teaching that children and cats are inherently incompatible
rather than manageable with simple supervision.

**Support type:** `SYNTHESIS` — CH2-04, CH2-05, and CH2-06 establish the
underlying signal-reading and letting-her-lead principles; applying them
specifically to a child-supervision context is this course's own reasoned
extension, not a claim any of those sources make about children. Disclosed
here per `CLAUDE.md` §19.

**Misconception tested:** That the fix for "a person might not read her
signals well" is either full unsupervised contact (A) or full exclusion
(C), rather than a supervising adult setting a simple enforceable rule (B).

## Common misconceptions

1. Initiation is a standing permission, not a moment-by-moment signal that
   needs re-checking (`CH2-05`, `CH2-06`).
2. A missed sensitive period means it no longer matters how kittenhood
   interactions go (`CH2-01`–`CH2-03`).
3. Play-biting is harmless because the kitten is small, regardless of what
   it's paired with (`CH2-07`).
4. A carrier or handling session should only happen when it's already
   urgently needed (e.g. right before a vet trip), rather than practised
   calmly in advance (`CH2-08`).
5. A person unlikely to reliably read or respect a withdrawal signal (e.g.
   a young child) needs to be taught nuanced signal-reading on the spot,
   rather than having a supervising adult set and enforce one simple rule
   in advance (secondary application case, `SYNTHESIS`).

  person against feline-friendly-handling principles.
- **Momo's state/age change:** Momo is now confidently approaching and

  patterns (play, carrier practice) beginning to form.
- **What becomes possible next:** Chapter 3 can assume the student already

## Placement justification

**Why here:** this is the only chapter in the entire twelve-chapter course
where the sensitive-period content (`CH2-01`–`CH2-03`) is *actionable* —
the window described is, by the course's own timeline, closing during
these exact teaching weeks. **Progression/swap test:** unlike some
environment-design content, this chapter cannot be swapped with a later
chapter without becoming false — placed in, say, Part III, it would be
teaching a window that has already closed for this specific cat, turning
live guidance into historical trivia. **What is lost if removed:** the
course would jump from "don't force interaction" (Chapter 1's blunt rule)
straight to "read for health concerns" (Chapter 3) with no chapter that
actually teaches how to read and respond to her signals in ordinary,
low-stakes moments — leaving the "observer" stage of the course's owner
progression (`docs/CURRICULUM.md`) asserted rather than actually taught.
