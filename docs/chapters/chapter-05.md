## Teaching purpose

This chapter corrects three specific misconceptions, each attached to a
behaviour owners commonly mislabel:

1. **"She's scratching the couch to be spiteful / to get back at me."**
   Scratching is not corrected by punishment, because it was never
   disobedience. It is territorial and identity marking — a normal
   behaviour performed in the wrong place, not a normal behaviour with an
   abnormal motive.
2. **"She pounces on my feet / attacks my hands because she's being
   aggressive."** This is very often the predatory sequence — a hunting
   behaviour cats retain regardless of hunger — misdirected onto a person
   because nothing else is available to receive it.
3. **"She's licking that spot a lot, but cats groom themselves — it's not
   a big deal."** Overgrooming can be exactly that, or it can be masking
   a medical cause the owner cannot see. The two look identical from the
   couch. Assuming it's "just grooming" is the same error, in the other
   direction, as assuming it's automatically stress.

**Audience note (per `CLAUDE.md` §18).** The three behaviours taught here
occur across single-cat and multi-cat homes, in cats of any life stage,
and regardless of the household's income or the amount of floor space
available — so this chapter draws its scenario variation from *household
resources* (a genuine, common constraint) rather than from any invented
age, ability, or family-structure assumption.

## Prerequisite capability

This chapter builds directly on two earlier capabilities:

- **Chapter 1** (`CH1-09`): the daily observation log gives the owner a
  personal baseline for what Momo normally does, which is what makes a
  *change* in scratching, play intensity, or grooming noticeable at all.
- **Chapter 2** (`CH2-07`): play should already be redirected onto toys
  rather than hands/feet. This chapter does not re-teach that rule — it
  supplies the reason behind it (the predatory sequence) and extends the
  same redirect-not-punish logic to two more behaviours.
- **Chapter 3** (`CH3-03`, `CH3-04`): house-soiling already established
  two principles this chapter deliberately reuses rather than
  reinvents — never punish a need-driven behaviour, and reason
  medical-first when a behaviour could have a physical cause. Chapter 5
  is the first place the student sees these two principles were never
  really about litter boxes at all.
- **Chapter 4** (`CH4-04`): the combined-signal-reading skill is used
  directly in Cluster C to distinguish playful predatory arousal from
  overstimulated/anxious arousal — the same pounce requires a different
  response depending on what the body language alongside it shows, so
  this chapter is not only about what to *do* once a behaviour is
  noticed, but sometimes about reading it correctly enough to know which
  response applies.

## Concrete knowledge map

### Cluster A — The reframe: need-driven, not naughty

- **`CH5-04`** *(SYNTHESIS)*: Across scratching, predatory-play
  frustration, and overgrooming, one pattern recurs: what looks like a
  "problem" is very often an unmet species-typical need expressed the
  only way the cat has available — or, in overgrooming's case, may be
  masking a medical cause. The effective response is to identify and meet
  the need in an acceptable form (or rule out a medical cause first), not
  to suppress or punish the behaviour directly.
  - *Owner relevance:* This is the one idea the whole chapter is built to
    install. Everything below is a worked example of it.
  - *Misconception corrected:* That "problem behaviours" are a single
    category needing a single kind of firmness.
  - *Callback:* Explicitly named as the same underlying principle behind
    `CH3-04` (no punishment for house-soiling) and `CH3-03` (medical-first
    reasoning), now recognised as general rather than litter-box-specific.
  - *Scope note:* This is a course-constructed synthesis, not a single
    guideline's own statement — disclosed per `CLAUDE.md` §19.

### Cluster B — Scratching

- **`CH5-01`**: Scratching is species-typical territorial/identity
  marking (scent-gland deposition via paw glands, plus claw-sheath
  removal) — not spite, revenge, or disobedience. Punishment fails
  because the cat does not link the punishment to the act, and can add
  stress that worsens the behaviour. Effective response: identify the
  preferred surface/orientation (vertical vs horizontal, rough vs woven
  texture), provide a matching alternative at least as tall/long as the
  cat stretched out, placed *near* the existing scratch site, then
  gradually relocated if needed; reduce the off-limits surface's appeal
  (blocking access, deterrent texture, scent-neutralising the site).
  Regular nail trims/caps reduce damage but do not replace a scratching
  outlet. Declawing is a last-resort, invasive, amputation-equivalent
  procedure — not a proportionate first response to a normal behaviour.
  - *Owner relevance:* Directly answers "why does she scratch the couch
    and not the post I already bought?" (wrong surface, wrong location,
    or wrong orientation — not defiance).
  - *Misconception corrected:* Scratching-as-spite; declawing as a
    reasonable first-line fix.
  - *Callback:* Direct extension of the no-punishment principle from
    `CH3-04`.

### Cluster C — Predatory play

- **`CH5-02`**: Cats retain a strong drive to perform the predatory
  sequence (locate → stalk → chase → pounce → "kill" → eat) regardless of
  hunger or how well-fed they are. Owners should let the cat express this
  via wand/rod-toy play that mimics prey and simulates a capture,
  food-foraging methods (hiding food in multiple spots, scatter-feeding,
  puzzle/timed feeders), and toy rotation to prevent habituation — and
  should avoid hands/feet as play objects. Recognising the sequence is
  not enough on its own: the same pounce can be playful arousal or
  overstimulated/anxious arousal, and telling them apart requires
  reading body language alongside the behaviour (ears forward and
  relaxed, soft-focus eyes, and a quick disengage after contact suggest
  play; ears pinned back, dilated pupils held rigidly, and a lashing
  rather than swishing tail suggest overstimulation) — the same
  positive/inhibition/avoidance/repelling signal families already
  taught in `CH2-05` and practised in combination in `CH4-04`. Playful
  pouncing should be redirected onto a toy; overstimulated pouncing
  should instead be met by disengaging and letting arousal settle, since
  more play at that point escalates rather than resolves it.
  - *Owner relevance:* Explains why a well-fed cat still stalks, pounces
    on feet under a blanket, or "attacks" ankles — this is not
    aggression or a broken temperament, it is an unmet, entirely normal
    behavioural need — and gives the owner a way to tell a playful
    pounce from one that needs space instead of a toy.
  - *Misconception corrected:* Pouncing/"attacking" as aggression;
    feeding more or feeding differently as an unrelated fix; treating
    every pounce as identical regardless of what the cat's body language
    shows alongside it.
  - *Callback:* Supplies the motivational reason behind Chapter 2's
    hands/feet redirect rule (`CH2-07`); requires genuinely applying
    `CH2-05`'s body-language signal families and `CH4-04`'s
    combined-signal reading to distinguish play from overstimulation,
    not just citing that skill in passing; connects to Chapter 1's
    feeding-log habit (`CH1-09`) as a place foraging methods could be
    logged and noticed.

### Cluster D — Overgrooming

- **`CH5-03`**: Overgrooming requires the same medical-first,
  diagnostic-elimination reasoning as house-soiling. Physical causes
  (e.g. a localised pain source such as disc disease or anal-sac
  impaction, or a more widespread cause such as parasites, allergies, or
  a neurological condition) must be ruled out by a vet before treating
  the behaviour as stress/displacement-driven. Pain-related licking tends
  to stay localised to one spot; itch-related licking is more spread
  out. The behaviour can become self-reinforcing (licking damages skin →
  skin becomes infected → more licking) and can persist as a habit even
  after the original trigger resolves. Once medical causes are ruled out,
  owner-level responses include reducing stress via gradual transitions
  and familiar bedding, adding vertical retreat space, and ~10–15
  minutes/day of interactive play; medication is a vet-led decision only.
  - *Owner relevance:* Explains why "it's probably just stress" and
    "it's probably just grooming" are both premature — a bald, raw, or
    localised patch needs a vet visit first, not a guess.
  - *Misconception corrected:* That grooming behaviour is self-evidently
    either "normal" or "just stress," never medical.
  - *Callback:* Deliberately reuses Chapter 3's medical-first pattern
    (`CH3-03`), applied here to a new presenting behaviour rather than
    re-derived from scratch.
  - *Scope note:* Medication content is taught strictly as "a vet may
    consider this," never as an owner-actionable step, consistent with
    this course's medical scope boundary.

## Owner-facing application

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to look at a
behaviour that reads as "she's being difficult" and ask, in order: *what
need might this be meeting, or could this be medical?* — then choose a
response that matches the answer, rather than reaching for the same
response (more toys, a stern word, ignoring it) regardless of which
behaviour they're looking at.

## Opening scenario

**What the student sees:** Momo, now about five months old, has started
scratching the corner of a housemate's new sofa — the same corner, every
evening, right after her housemates get home. One housemate wants to
scold her or spray her with water when caught in the act. Another says a
friend "just got their cat declawed and it solved the problem instantly,"
and wonders if that's the easier fix.

**What is deliberately withheld:** Whether Momo has an existing
scratching post, and if so, where it is and what it's made of. The
student is being invited to reach for a *response* (punish, declaw)
before anyone has asked the more basic diagnostic question.

**Decision the student must make:** What should actually happen the next
time Momo scratches the sofa corner?

## Opening question

| Option | Text | Intended? | Why tempting | Misconception revealed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Scold her or spray her with water when caught scratching — cats learn from correction | No | Feels immediate, feels like "training," matches how some other pet behaviours are corrected | Assumes scratching is a trainable-away misbehaviour rather than a normal need expressed in the wrong place; ignores that cats don't link the punishment to the act |
| B | Work out where and how she likes to scratch, put a proper post or pad in that exact spot, and make the sofa corner less appealing — without punishing her | **Yes** | Requires more thought and no immediate "consequence" for the cat, which can feel unsatisfying | None — this is the response the chapter is built to install |
| C | Look into declawing, since it "permanently solves" the problem in one step | No | A single procedure sounds cleaner and more final than an ongoing management routine | Treats an invasive, amputation-equivalent surgical procedure as a proportionate first response to a normal behaviour |

## Practice/applied activity

**Triage exercise.** The student is given six short behaviour vignettes
and must sort each into one of four response categories: *environmental
redirect*, *behavioural redirect* (toy/play-based), *vet rule-out first*,
or *immediate escalation* (reusing Chapter 3's escalation signs, `CH3-06`,
as a deliberate distractor category).

1. A cat scratches a doorframe daily. → Environmental redirect.
2. A cat pounces on ankles most evenings around dusk. → Behavioural
   redirect.
3. A cat has a small, raw, localised bald patch on one flank that wasn't
   there a week ago. → Vet rule-out first.
4. A cat is straining repeatedly in the litter box, crying, and hasn't
   produced urine in several hours. → Immediate escalation (this is
   `CH3-06`'s obstruction-risk signature, not a Chapter 5 behaviour at
   all — included specifically to test whether the student can tell the
   difference between "needs a response plan" and "needs a vet today").
5. A cat has started scratching a new houseplant's pot rather than any
   furniture. → Environmental redirect (same logic as `CH5-01`, applied
   to a different surface — deliberately not a furniture example, so the
   student generalises the *reasoning*, not the specific case).
6. A cat grooms normally for roughly a third of the day, with no visible
   skin change. → No action needed — this is normal grooming, not
   overgrooming; included to test whether the student over-applies
   `CH5-03` to ordinary behaviour.

This activity is deliberately not all-scratching or all-grooming — it
forces the student to hold all three domains (plus the Chapter 3
escalation category, plus a "nothing is wrong here" case) in mind at
once, which is the actual skill being taught.

## Transfer scenario/Case Check (primary)

**Scenario.** Over the course of one week, three things happen: Momo
scratches the same sofa corner despite a scratching post already sitting
nearby; she starts pouncing on the student's feet most nights; and the
student notices a small, raw, bald patch developing on her left flank
from licking. A housemate proposes a single response to all three:
"she's stressed — let's just get her more toys and see if it settles
down."

| Option | Text |
|---|---|
| A | Treat all three the same way — she's stressed, so add more toys generally and wait to see if things improve |
| B | Work out what each behaviour is actually telling you: check whether the existing post matches her preferred surface/location and adjust if not; redirect the night-pouncing onto wand-toy play before bed; and get the licked patch checked by a vet before assuming it's just stress |
| C | Since she's already fed and has toys, these must simply be misbehaviour, and she should be firmly corrected each time she does one of them |

**Answer:** B

**Reasoning:** A sounds caring but is not actually diagnostic — it
applies one generic fix ("more toys") to three specific and different
needs, and critically, it skips the one behaviour here that is not
simply need-driven: the bald patch requires ruling out a medical cause
first, not just more enrichment, no matter how well-intentioned. C
repeats the Chapter 3/4 punishment misconception in blanket form,
treating need-driven behaviour as deliberate misbehaviour regardless of
which behaviour is involved. B is the only option that does two things
at once: applies a matched, behaviour-specific fix to the two
environmental/behavioural needs (the post's placement, and the
foot-pouncing), *and* correctly recognises that the licked patch is
qualitatively different from the other two — it needs the medical-first
reasoning from Chapter 3, not another enrichment fix. This is the core
skill this chapter is teaching: not a single rule to apply everywhere,
but a triage step that comes before any rule is chosen.

## Secondary application case

**Pedagogical variable:** where the primary case tests differentiating
*between* need-driven and medical behaviours, this case tests applying
`CH5-01` under a genuine household resource constraint — a real
variation many households face (per `CLAUDE.md` §18), not an assumption
about any particular kind of household.

**Scenario.** A housemate says a proper scratching post or cat tree is
too expensive right now, and suggests that declawing might actually work
out cheaper in the long run since it's "a one-time vet cost" versus an
ongoing furniture-replacement problem.

| Option | Text |
|---|---|
| A | Agree to look into declawing, since it's framed as a one-time cost that solves the problem permanently |
| B | Point out that an effective scratching outlet doesn't require an expensive product — a sturdy DIY option (e.g. a rope-wrapped board, a flattened cardboard scratcher, a secured fence post offcut) placed correctly at the existing scratch site can work just as well — and that declawing is an invasive, last-resort procedure, not a proportionate response to a cost question |
| C | Accept that a "real" scratching post is unaffordable right now, and just let the sofa keep getting damaged |
| — | **Answer: B** |

**Reasoning:** This case deliberately does not invent a "type" of
household — a budget constraint is common and real, and the correct
response does not require the student to assume the family is unable to
provide any solution (as in C), nor does it accept an invasive surgical
procedure as a reasonable trade-off for cost (as in A). B shows that
`CH5-01`'s underlying mechanism (matching surface, orientation, and
location) does not depend on an expensive product — the *redirect*
principle is what matters, not the price tag. This contrasts with the
primary case's variable (differentiating need-driven from medical
behaviour) by testing a different failure mode entirely: substituting an
invasive "solution" for a genuine but solvable resource constraint.

**Support type note:** The specific DIY material examples in option B
(rope-wrapped board, cardboard scratcher, fence post offcut) are not
verbatim recommendations from `S26` — they are reasonable, low-cost
instantiations of `S26`'s general "provide a matching alternative"
principle, disclosed here as **SYNTHESIS** rather than presented as a
direct guideline recommendation, consistent with `CLAUDE.md` §19.

## Common misconceptions

1. Scratching is spite, revenge, or a sign the cat "knows" it's doing
   something wrong.
2. Punishing (scolding, water-spraying) a need-driven behaviour will
   train it away.
3. Declawing is a reasonable, low-stakes first response to a scratching
   problem.
4. Pouncing on feet/hands is aggression rather than the normal predatory
   sequence looking for an outlet.
5. Any grooming-related behaviour is automatically either "just
   grooming" or "just stress" — never a possible signal of a medical
   cause requiring a vet.
6. All three behaviours in this chapter call for the same kind of
   response, so once you've handled one you can handle them all the same
   way.

## Chapter completion

**Capability gained:** The student can look at scratching, predatory
play, or overgrooming and identify which of three responses is
appropriate — environmental redirect, behavioural redirect, or
medical-first escalation — rather than applying punishment, a single
generic fix, or a resigned "that's just how cats are" to all of them
alike.

**Momo's state/age change:** Momo moves from ~16 weeks (Chapter 4) to
~5–6 months, entering the period where adult-typical behaviours
(scratching intensity, hunting drive, self-grooming patterns) become
fully established — the natural moment for these behaviours to first
become visible enough to misinterpret.

**What becomes possible next:** Chapter 6 depends on the student already
being able to recognise *what* a behaviour is (Chapter 4) and *what kind
of response it needs* (Chapter 5) in order to teach the harder skill of
noticing when something has *changed* against Momo's personal baseline —
Chapter 5's triage categories (environmental / behavioural / medical) are
reused directly as the "what changed, and what should I do about it"
framework Chapter 6 builds on.

## Placement justification

**Why here:** This chapter cannot precede Chapter 4, because triaging a
behaviour depends on first being able to read it accurately (does she
seem tense, aroused, relaxed? — `CH4-04`'s combined-signal skill). It
also could not precede Chapter 3, since two of its four claims
(`CH5-03`, `CH5-04`) are explicit reuses of Chapter 3's medical-first and
no-punishment reasoning — teaching them here without that foundation
would require re-deriving both principles from scratch, duplicating
content rather than transferring it.

**Progression-swap test:** If Chapter 5 were moved before Chapter 4, the
student would be asked to triage behaviours they cannot yet reliably
read — the "is she stressed or just playing?" judgment calls throughout
this chapter depend on Chapter 4's combined-signal-reading skill. If
moved after Chapter 6, the capstone "what changed?" reasoning in Chapter
6 would have nothing concrete to reuse, since it is Chapter 5's
environmental/behavioural/medical triage categories that give Chapter 6
its structure.

**What is lost if removed:** Without this chapter, the course would teach
observation (Chapters 1–4) without ever teaching what to *do* with an
observed behaviour beyond "notice it" — leaving the punishment
misconception unaddressed for three extremely common, real-world
"problem behaviours" that new owners specifically encounter once a cat
reaches this age, and removing the direct callback bridge Chapter 11
needs to reuse Chapter 3's medical-first reasoning a second time
(Chapter 5 is the chapter that first shows the student this reasoning
pattern is general, not litter-box-specific — priming its later reuse in
Chapter 11).
