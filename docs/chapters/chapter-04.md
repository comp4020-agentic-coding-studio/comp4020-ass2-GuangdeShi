## Teaching purpose

Chapter 2 taught the student to read body language in the moment — the
positive/inhibition/avoidance/repelling framework — for handling and
consent. That was enough to answer "is contact currently welcome?" It is
not enough to answer the much broader question this chapter opens with:
once a student can already read a cat's body language, what does it mean
when she also vocalises, or scent-marks, and the message isn't about
whether to touch her at all? Three linked misconceptions are addressed
here:

1. That a single vocal sound has one fixed meaning — that a yowl "means"
   something specific the way a word does — rather than being one
   ambiguous signal among several that must be read together.
2. That scent-marking behaviours (scratching, rubbing against legs or
   furniture, occasional marking) are meaningless quirks or the absence of
   communication, rather than a whole second communication channel running
   alongside body language and vocalisation.
3. That an owner who is confident about what a vocalisation means is
   therefore correct — confidence is not the same as accuracy, and this
   chapter introduces direct evidence that even attentive, experienced
   owners are frequently wrong when judging a single cue alone.

## Prerequisite capability

Chapter 2: the student already reads a cat's body-language signal family
(positive/inhibition/avoidance/repelling) in the moment and runs a
consent-check before and during handling. Chapter 4 does not repeat that
framework — it adds two new channels (vocal, scent) on top of it, and
teaches the combined-reading skill of using all of them together, rather
than reading body language alone.

## Concrete knowledge map

### Cluster A — The vocal vocabulary

- **Three broad categories of vocal sound**: murmur/closed-mouth sounds
  (purring, used from kitten-nursing through adult greeting and
  allogrooming contexts; the trill/chirrup — a short "ppprrrrrrp" — used
  as a friendly greeting and, by mothers, as a kitten contact call);
  meowing (an extensive, variable range in pitch, intensity and duration,
  produced mainly toward people — genuinely rare between adult cats — and
  likely shaped over time by how people respond to it); and hostile
  open-mouth sounds (growls, yowls, snarls, hisses, spits, shrieks, wails)
  in offensive or defensive encounters (`CH4-01`). *Owner relevance:* a
  meow is, structurally, a signal a cat has learned gets a person's
  attention — not a single word with a single dictionary meaning.
- **Meows are individually variable, not standardised** — the same cat may
  use noticeably different meows for different situations, but there is
  no universal "meow dictionary" that translates reliably across cats or
  even always within one cat (`CH4-01`, `CH4-03`).

### Cluster B — The scent vocabulary

- **Bunting (rubbing)**: rubbing the face, cheek, or body against a
  person or object deposits scent from glands concentrated around the
  chin, cheeks, forehead, and tail base; it happens mainly in the cat's
  core territory and is associated with comfort, reassurance, and
  friendly social interaction — this is the same behaviour as leg-rubbing
  a person, redirected because a person's face isn't reachable (`CH4-02`).
- **Scratching carries a scent message, not just a visible one** — paw
  glands mean a scratch leaves a chemical mark alongside the visible
  claw-marks, typically along routes the cat travels often rather than at
  territorial edges (`CH4-02`). *Callback:* this connects directly to the
  no-punishment principle Chapter 3 already established for house-soiling
  (`CH3-04`) — scratching is marking behaviour, not disobedience, and
  Chapter 5 will build the owner's *response* to this on top of
  recognising it here first.
- **Urine and faeces placement can itself be a signal**: buried within
  core territory, left more visibly at territorial edges — placement, not
  just presence, carries information (`CH4-02`). *Scope note:* this
  chapter teaches recognition only; Chapter 5 covers what an owner should
  actually do about a marking or house-soiling concern.

### Cluster C — Why one cue, alone, is not enough

- **A single meow heard without context is genuinely hard to interpret
  correctly** — in a peer-reviewed study, adult listeners (including cat
  owners) classifying an isolated meow's situation never reached accuracy
  meaningfully above chance, even for the clearest recordings (`CH4-03`).
  *Misconception corrected:* this is not evidence that meowing is
  meaningless — it is evidence that the sound alone, stripped of context,
  underdetermines the meaning.
- **Emotional tone survives even when specific meaning doesn't**:
  listeners in the same study could often sense whether a meow was
  generally positive or negative, even when they couldn't identify the
  exact situation (`CH4-03`). *Owner relevance:* "something's off" is a
  more reliable read from sound alone than "she specifically wants X."
- **Experience helps, but only partially** — owners were modestly more
  accurate than non-owners, but even they were far from reliable on sound
  alone (`CH4-03`). *Misconception corrected:* knowing your own cat well
  does not license confident single-cue interpretation; it should instead
  make an owner more careful to check the surrounding context, not less.

### Cluster D — Reading the combination, not the cue

- **The combined-reading method**: when a cat vocalises or scent-marks,
  interpret it together with her current body-language signal family
  (Chapter 2), what she is physically doing (moving toward what?), and the
  situational context (time of day, what just happened, what usually
  happens next) — never the vocal or scent cue in isolation (`CH4-04`,
  `SYNTHESIS`). *Owner relevance:* this converts "what does that meow
  mean?" from an unanswerable question about one sound into an answerable
  question about a pattern of several things happening together.
- **A household "story" is not verification** — once people agree on an
  explanation for a recurring vocalisation ("she always does that because
  X"), the agreement itself can feel like proof, even if no one has
  actually checked it against her current signals (`CH4-04`, applied).
  *Misconception corrected:* consensus between people is not the same as
  evidence from the cat.

## Owner-facing application

By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to: name what
category a vocal sound roughly falls into and what that category
typically means; recognise scratching, rubbing, and marking behaviour as
communication rather than misbehaviour or randomness; resist the urge to
assign one fixed meaning to an isolated vocalisation; and, when Momo
vocalises or scent-marks, deliberately check her body language and the
surrounding situation before deciding what she is likely communicating.

## Opening scenario

**Status: PROPOSED.**

It's early evening. Momo trots to the front door, yowls loudly, and paces
back and forth. One housemate says it's obvious — she wants to go
outside. Another disagrees — dinner is usually served around now, so she's
probably just hungry and headed the wrong way.

What the student sees: one loud, unmistakable vocal signal, and two
confident but conflicting explanations for it.

What is deliberately withheld: which explanation (if either) is correct —
the point is that the sound alone does not settle it.

Decision the student must make: how to actually work out what Momo wants,
rather than pick whichever explanation sounds most confident.

## Practice / applied activity

**Status: PROPOSED (description only — no UI/interaction implementation at
this stage).** The student works through five short described vignettes
(text/still-image, not video), each presenting a vocal or scent cue
alongside partial body-language and situational information. For each, the
student states what channel(s) of information are present, what a
single-cue read would tempt someone to conclude, and what the combined
read actually supports — including at least one vignette where the
combined read genuinely remains ambiguous and the correct answer is "keep
watching, don't act on this alone yet," to avoid teaching that the method
always produces a confident answer.

## Transfer scenario / Case Check

**Status: PROPOSED draft.**

**Scenario:** A visiting cousin, hearing Momo yowl at the door as the
household is getting ready for dinner, says confidently: "She clearly
wants to go outside — you should let her."

**Options:**
- A. Agree and let her outside, since the cousin sounds sure and the yowl was loud and clear.
- B. Check her body language and situation first — is she facing the door or angling toward the kitchen, what do her tail and ears show, and is this close to her usual feeding time — before deciding what to do.
- C. Tell the cousin cats can't communicate anything reliable through sound, so the yowling should just be ignored.

**Answer:** B.

**Reasoning:** Distinct from Chapter 2's transfer case (which asked how to
conduct an *already-agreed* interaction with a visitor). This asks a
different question: given a confident but unverified interpretation of a
vocal signal, how should the owner actually decide what's true? A repeats
this chapter's core misconception in a new guise (a confident-sounding
person substituting for the actual combined signals); C repeats the
opening question's over-correction (treating vocalisation as inherently
meaningless). B is the only option that applies the chapter's actual new
content — combining vocal cue, body language, and situational context —
rather than deferring to whoever sounds most certain.

**Misconception tested:** That someone else's confident interpretation of
a single vocal cue is a substitute for the owner's own combined read of
body language, scent behaviour, and situation.

## Secondary application case

**Status: PROPOSED draft.** Per `CLAUDE.md` §18, this chapter pairs the
primary transfer case above with a second, deliberately different real
household situation — not for variety's own sake, but because it surfaces
a different variable: what happens when the person who has to decide
wasn't there to observe directly.

**Scenario:** A housemate who works night shifts gets home as the
household is discussing what happened earlier — Momo pacing and
vocalising at the door before dinner. They didn't see it, and the two
people who did still disagree about what it meant.

**Options:**
- A. Go with whichever account sounds more detailed or confident, since a firsthand description is better than nothing.
- B. Check the shared daily log for what was actually recorded (time, what Momo was doing, whether she'd been fed) and use that, alongside asking both housemates what her body language specifically looked like, rather than picking a side.
- C. Since it's already over and can't be re-observed, decide it doesn't matter and move on without drawing any conclusion.

**Answer:** B.

**Reasoning:** This is not a claim that a person who wasn't present is
less capable of judgement than one who was — the actual variable is
**what information is available for the combined read once real-time
observation isn't possible.** The housemate in this case cannot personally
check Momo's body language in the moment (unlike the cousin case, where
combined-reading information is available live); what *is* available is
the shared daily log established in Chapter 1 (`CH1-09`) — a concrete
record of time, feeding status, and behaviour that substitutes for
firsthand observation. A repeats the "confident account wins" error from
the primary case in a new guise (detail/confidence standing in for
verification); C over-corrects into treating a missed observation as
unrecoverable, when a recorded log entry is exactly the tool this course
already established for this situation.

**Support type:** `SYNTHESIS` — `CH4-04` establishes the combined-reading
method; applying it specifically to a situation where firsthand
observation is unavailable, and identifying the daily log (`CH1-09`) as
the substitute information source, is this course's own reasoned
extension connecting two previously separate chapters, not a claim either
source makes directly. Disclosed here per `CLAUDE.md` §19.

**Misconception tested:** That when real-time combined-signal reading
isn't possible, the choices are either trust the most confident secondhand
account or give up on interpretation entirely — rather than using a
recorded observation tool that already exists for exactly this situation.

## Common misconceptions

1. A vocal sound has one fixed, dictionary-style meaning that can be read
   from the sound alone (`CH4-01`, `CH4-03`).
2. Scratching, rubbing, and marking behaviours are meaningless quirks
   rather than a real communication channel running alongside body
   language and vocalisation (`CH4-02`).
3. Confidence (the owner's own, or someone else's) is evidence of an
   interpretation's accuracy (`CH4-03`, primary transfer case).
4. The correct response to "single cues are unreliable" is to stop
   listening to vocalisation altogether, rather than to read it in
   combination with other signals (`CH4-04`, opening question option C).
5. When firsthand observation wasn't possible, the only options are to
   trust whoever sounds most certain or to give up on interpretation
   entirely, rather than using a recorded observation as a substitute
   (secondary application case, `SYNTHESIS`).

## Placement justification

**Why here:** this chapter is the first point in the course where the
student has both a body-language foundation (Chapter 2) and a settled,
communicative cat (several weeks in) to apply it to — placed earlier, the
vocal/scent vocabulary and the single-cue-unreliability evidence would
have nothing yet to attach to; placed later (e.g. alongside Chapter 5), it
would force Chapter 5 to teach both "what is this signal" and "how should
I respond" simultaneously, muddying a needs-response chapter with
vocabulary-building content it doesn't need to repeat. **Progression/swap
test:** this chapter cannot be swapped with Chapter 5 without creating a
dependency problem — Chapter 5's "needs, not naughtiness" reasoning
(distinguishing a need-driven behaviour from a "problem") depends on the
student already being able to recognise what a behaviour is communicating,
which is exactly this chapter's capability. **What is lost if removed:**
the course would jump from body-language handling (Chapter 2) straight to
behavioural-response design (Chapter 5) without ever teaching the
recognition step in between — leaving students to design responses to
signals they were never actually taught to read correctly, and removing
the course's one explicit lesson in epistemic humility about
interpretation (that confidence is not accuracy).
