## Teaching purpose

This chapter exists to correct the one misconception the course itself just
spent two chapters building: that watching and waiting is always the right
first move. Chapters 1 and 2 taught patience and observation as the
default. Chapter 3 teaches the necessary exception — because an owner who
has only ever learned "give her time" has no way to recognise the specific,
narrow set of situations where time is exactly what she doesn't have.
Three linked misconceptions:

1. That any change in litter box behaviour is either "just the litter box"
   (fixable by switching brands or location) or "just settling in" —
   rather than something that should be checked against medical causes
   first.
2. That a cat should be corrected or punished for eliminating outside the
   box, when punishment does not address the cause and can make a
   stress-related one worse.
3. That "watch and see" is a safe default for *any* symptom — when a small
   number of specific presentations (most importantly, repeated
   unproductive straining or crying while trying to urinate) should
   override that default entirely and prompt same-day veterinary contact.

## Prerequisite capability

Chapter 1's daily observation log is what makes this chapter's central
judgement possible at all — "is this normal for her?" is only answerable
with a baseline to compare against. Chapter 2's body-language literacy
means the student is already paying close, continuous attention to her,
not just glancing over occasionally.

## Concrete knowledge map

### Cluster A — Setting up and reading the litter box correctly

- **Box setup**: at least one box per cat plus one extra; large (roughly
  1.5× her eventual adult nose-to-tail length); fine, unscented, clumping
  litter at least ~3cm deep; scoop at least daily; wash with soap and hot
  water every 1–4 weeks; keep away from food/water and not facing another
  box; once she reliably uses a box, don't relocate it for at least two
  weeks (`CH3-01`).
- **Marking vs. toileting vs. location preference are different
  problems**: marking is small-volume, on vertical surfaces, from a
  standing posture with the tail up and twitching, while the box is still
  used normally for full voids; toileting-outside-the-box is full-volume
  and often about the box itself; location preference is different again
  (`CH3-02`). *Owner relevance:* the fix depends entirely on which pattern
  is actually happening — "just move the box" is only sometimes the right
  answer.

### Cluster B — Medical-first reasoning, and never punishing

- **Litter box problems can be medical, behavioural, or both at once** —
  a medical cause (e.g. feline idiopathic cystitis or another urinary tract
  issue) should never be ruled out just because an environmental cause also
  seems plausible, and treating the medical cause alone will not
  necessarily fix a learned aversion that developed alongside it (`CH3-03`).
- **Never punish elimination outside the box** — it doesn't address the
  cause, and worsens a stress-related one (`CH3-04`).

### Cluster C — Concrete "call the vet" signs

- **A specific, memorable list**, not a vague feeling: dull/patchy coat,
  appetite loss, persistent vomiting/diarrhoea, nasal/eye discharge or
  sneezing, unexplained weight change, urinary straining or blood in
  urine, excessive thirst, breathing difficulty, lethargy, or unexplained
  swelling (`CH3-05`). *Owner relevance:* replaces "I have a bad feeling"
  with a checklist the student can actually apply to their own log.

### Cluster D — The exception to "wait and observe"

- **Some presentations override the default entirely.** Repeated
  unproductive straining to urinate, crying or vocalising while trying to
  eliminate, a firm and painful abdomen, or producing no urine at all
  should prompt same-day or emergency veterinary contact, not another day
  of watching (`CH3-06`).
- **What the source actually supports, stated precisely:** S7 states that
  urethral obstruction is a true emergency that "almost exclusively"
  affects male cats, because of their narrower urethra, and that it can
  become life-threatening within hours of onset. S7 does not report a
  comparable obstruction rate in female cats, but it also does not state
  that female cats are risk-free — its claim is a population-level pattern
  in male cats specifically, not a blanket statement about females.
- **Population-level risk vs. this individual cat — preserved, not
  smoothed over:** Momo is female. The population-level male-skew S7
  documents means the single most stereotypical cause of this presentation
  (complete urethral blockage) is materially less likely for her than it
  would be for a male cat showing the same signs — that real difference is
  kept, not erased for simplicity. But a population-level pattern is not a
  certainty about one animal, and it does not clear her of every cause of
  this presentation: the sex-neutral cause (feline idiopathic cystitis,
  uncomfortable but not itself immediately life-threatening) remains fully
  possible for a cat of either sex, and at-home observation cannot
  distinguish it from the male-skewed emergency cause either.
- **The owner-facing boundary stays recognise → record → escalate, never
  diagnose:** the chapter does not ask the student to determine which
  cause is occurring — an owner cannot do that at home regardless of how
  much they know about relative risk by sex. It asks the student to
  recognise the presentation, record exactly what was observed, and
  escalate to same-day veterinary contact regardless of sex, because the
  two possible causes cannot be told apart without veterinary examination,
  and a delay is only ever costly in whichever case turns out to be the
  emergency one.

## Owner-facing application

By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to: set up a litter
box correctly the first time; describe, from their own log, what would
count as "different from her normal"; recite the concrete illness-warning
list without needing to look it up; and — the chapter's real test — decide,
under time pressure and without certainty, to call the vet today rather
than wait, when a specific narrow set of urinary signs appears.

## Opening scenario

**Status: PROPOSED.**

It's been about two and a half weeks. The student's log (started in
Chapter 1) shows Momo has been eating, drinking, and playing normally.
Yesterday, though, she went to the litter box more often than usual but
seemed to produce very little each time, and this morning the student
heard a short cry from the litter box. She's slightly quieter today, but
not dramatically different.

What the student sees: two small, easy-to-explain-away signals (more trips,
less output, one cry) against an otherwise-normal baseline.

What is deliberately withheld: whether anything is actually wrong — the
whole point is that the student must decide under genuine uncertainty, the
way this decision is actually made in real life.

Decision the student must make: what to do today.

## Practice / applied activity

**Status: PROPOSED (description only — no UI/interaction implementation at
this stage).** The student is given five short log excerpts (continuing the
format introduced in Chapter 1) describing a day in a hypothetical cat's
life, and must classify each as "consistent with settling in / normal
variation," "worth a routine vet mention," or "same-day vet contact" —
with a one-sentence justification per excerpt referencing the specific
sign(s) that drove the classification. At least one excerpt should be a
genuine distractor: a dramatic-sounding but actually low-urgency change
(e.g. one day of reduced appetite with no other signs), to test whether the
student has learned the specific criteria rather than a general anxiety
response.

## Transfer scenario / Case Check

**Status: PROPOSED draft.**

**Scenario:** Momo hasn't eaten in over 24 hours and seems more tired than
usual today. She is still using the litter box normally, with no visible
distress.

**Options:**
- A. Wait another day — kittens can be picky eaters, and nothing else seems wrong.
- B. Contact the vet today, describing the appetite loss and lethargy and how they compare to her usual logged baseline.
- C. Offer her a favourite treat to make sure she's getting some calories, and check again tomorrow.

**Answer:** B.

**Reasoning:** This is not a repeat of the opening question — it swaps the
urinary-sign cluster for an appetite/energy cluster, testing whether the
student generalised the *underlying* judgement (a specific, checklist-based
deviation from her own baseline crosses an escalation threshold) rather
than just memorising "watch for litter box problems." A repeats this
chapter's central misconception in a new guise; C is a well-meaning DIY
intervention that delays proper diagnosis rather than replacing the need
for it.

**Misconception tested:** That "nothing else seems wrong" is reassuring on
its own, rather than a specific, listed sign (`CH3-05`: appetite loss,
lethargy) already being sufficient by itself to warrant contact.

## Ambiguous case study (mixed evidence)

**Status: PROPOSED draft.** Unlike the opening question and the transfer
case above — both of which have one clearly best answer once the taught
criteria are applied — this case is designed to sit genuinely between the
chapter's two "easy" categories (normal variation vs. `CH3-06`'s absolute
emergency list), so the student has to weigh mixed evidence rather than
pattern-match to a memorised rule.

**Scenario:** A loud home-repair crew has been in and out of the house for
the past two days. Over the same two days, Momo has eaten only about half
her usual amount, seems to be drinking somewhat more water than her
Chapter-1 baseline log shows, and has been hiding under the bed for more of
the day than she has in weeks. Last night she gave one soft cry when picked
up. This morning she used the litter box and appeared to strain slightly
more than usual, but produced what looked like a normal amount of urine.

**Why this case is genuinely ambiguous, not a disguised easy case:** none
of `CH3-06`'s absolute red-flag criteria are met — she produced a normal
amount of urine (not "no urine at all"), the extra straining was described
as slight and observed once (not "repeated unproductive straining"), and
her cry happened on handling, not while eliminating. So the chapter's own
absolute-escalate rule, correctly applied, does **not** fire here. At the
same time, reduced appetite and increased thirst are each independently on
the concrete warning-sign list (`CH3-05`), a real environmental stressor
(the repair crew) is plausibly sufficient on its own to explain a
stress-and-hiding response, and several mild signs are co-occurring rather
than appearing in isolation. Nothing about this case required inventing a
new threshold — it is built entirely from `CH3-05`'s already-sourced
warning-sign list and `CH3-06`'s already-sourced red-flag list, used to
show what falls *between* them.

**Plausible responses (deliberately not reduced to one obviously-correct
click):**

- **Assume the repair crew fully explains it; change nothing and don't
  contact the vet**, since none of `CH3-06`'s emergency criteria are
  present. *Why tempting:* it correctly identifies a real, present
  stressor and correctly notes the absolute rule hasn't fired. *What it
  misses:* reduced appetite and increased thirst are independently listed,
  sufficient-on-their-own warning signs (`CH3-05`) — a plausible
  explanation for a cluster of signs is not the same as confirming that
  explanation, and several co-occurring mild signs deserve more than being
  silently attributed to noise.
- **Keep watching closely today, log the specifics precisely (exact
  amounts eaten and drunk, duration of hiding, exact litter box
  observation), and contact the vet today to describe the combination and
  ask whether it warrants a visit now or can be monitored** — continuing
  Chapter 1's log habit and looping in a vet proactively without treating
  it as an emergency. *This is the strongest response*: it neither ignores
  the `CH3-05`-listed signs (as the first option does) nor over-applies
  `CH3-06`'s absolute-emergency rule to a milder presentation than the one
  that rule actually describes (as the next option does).
- **Treat it as an emergency and seek same-day/urgent care immediately**,
  reasoning that "any litter box straining plus crying" should trigger
  `CH3-06`'s rule. *Why tempting:* it applies the chapter's own
  just-taught lesson (don't wait on urinary signs) as broadly as possible,
  which feels safe. *What it misses:* `CH3-06`'s rule was scoped narrowly
  on purpose — to *repeated, unproductive* straining, crying *while*
  eliminating, or *no* urine produced. Stretching it to cover a single
  instance of mild straining with normal output risks teaching the student
  to over-trigger the emergency rule on every mild stress episode, which
  would blunt its credibility for the times it is actually meant to fire.
- **Assume the litter box observation means an environmental/setup problem
  is developing, and change the litter box setup** (add a box, change
  litter type) without contacting the vet. *Why tempting:* it correctly
  applies Cluster A's setup knowledge. *What it misses:* it repeats this
  chapter's Cluster B misconception — reaching for an environmental fix
  before ruling out a medical explanation, when multiple signs are
  co-occurring rather than pointing cleanly at a setup issue.

**Support type: `SYNTHESIS`** (`CLAUDE.md` §19). No single source states
"this specific combination of signs warrants a same-day informational vet
call but not an emergency visit" — that judgement is assembled from two
already-registered, independently-sourced lists (`CH3-05`'s warning signs;
`CH3-06`'s narrowly-scoped absolute red flags) plus the reasoning that a
plausible non-medical explanation does not discharge an owner's
responsibility to still act on independently-listed warning signs. The
chapter should disclose this assembly to the student in the reveal/teach
material for this beat, rather than presenting it as though one guideline
directly prescribed this exact response.

**Misconception tested:** That every case must resolve cleanly to either
"ignore it" or "treat it as an emergency" — when real caregiving regularly
requires judgement calls in between, and the responsible move in the
ambiguous middle is usually *proactive information-sharing with a vet*,
not silence or panic.

## Common misconceptions

1. Any change in litter box behaviour is a "just the box" or "just
   settling in" problem rather than something to check medically first
   (`CH3-02`, `CH3-03`).
2. Punishing elimination outside the box addresses the problem (`CH3-04`).
3. "Wait and observe," which was correct throughout Chapters 1 and 2, is
   always correct — rather than being overridden by a specific, narrow set
   of urinary-distress signs (`CH3-06`).
4. A single symptom in isolation ("she's just a bit tired") isn't
   meaningful on its own, when several items on the concrete warning-sign
   list (`CH3-05`) are each independently sufficient to warrant contact.
5. Every situation must resolve to either "definitely fine" or "definitely
   an emergency" — rather than sometimes calling for proactive vet contact
   under real uncertainty, without either ignoring listed warning signs or
   over-applying the narrow emergency rule (ambiguous case study,
   `SYNTHESIS`).

## Placement justification

**Why here:** this chapter's central tension — "is this deviation just
settling in, or is it a real problem?" — only exists for a cat without an
established long-term baseline; it is Part I's specific ambiguity, not Part
III's. **Progression/swap test:** this chapter could not swap with a Part
III ("Keep Her Well") chapter about an established adult cat's preventive
care — that context has a different ambiguity (subtle, slow-developing
chronic disease in a cat whose "normal" is already well known), not the
"still settling in, so is this even a deviation" question this chapter is
built around. **What is lost if removed:** without it, the course's own
"instruction-following owner → observer" progression (`docs/CURRICULUM.md`)
would be missing its one direct lesson in overriding a just-taught default
— leaving "know when to seek veterinary advice," one of the course's
foundational owner-competence goals (`CLAUDE.md` §15's scope test: owner
competence, not veterinary training), untaught until much later, if at all.
