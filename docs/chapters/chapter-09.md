## Teaching purpose

This chapter corrects two opposite misconceptions, which is deliberate —
the same "wait and see" vs. "escalate everything" tension Chapter 6 named
resurfaces here in a harder form, because pain and dental discomfort are
specifically the kinds of problems a cat is built to conceal:

1. **"If she's still eating and using the litter box normally, nothing is
   wrong."** Cats routinely conceal pain and dental disease; the *absence*
   of dramatic symptoms is not evidence of the *absence* of a problem —
   it is the expected presentation.
2. **"One flinch, one squint, one sniff of bad breath means she's in
   serious pain — treat it as an emergency."** A single, momentary,
   context-explained sign is not the same as a persistent, unexplained
   change against baseline. Over-reading one ambiguous sign is its own
   failure mode, not a safer default than under-reading it.

**Audience note (per `CLAUDE.md` §18):** the practice activity in this
chapter varies how *often* and how *closely* different households
realistically observe their cat day to day (a person with an unpredictable
work schedule who mostly sees the cat in the evening, versus retired
housemates home most of the day) — because that changes how much of a
gradual, subtle change a household is actually likely to catch in real
time, not because either household is a better or worse caregiver. The
chapter's method (compare against a logged baseline, don't rely on memory
alone) is precisely what makes the capability workable regardless of how
much observation time a household has.

## Prerequisite capability

This chapter depends on two earlier chapters, and both dependencies are
directly exercised, not merely cited:

- **Chapter 6** (`CH6-01`, `CH6-02`): the four-step baseline-comparison
  method — recall baseline, identify what changed, check the
  urgent-escalation signature, then check for a benign explanation — is
  not re-derived here. It is reapplied, step by step, to facial pain
  signs and dental signs specifically (`CH9-07`). Chapter 6's two named
  failure modes (normalisation drift, over-escalation) are the exact two
  misconceptions this chapter targets, now in a domain where the signs
  themselves are quieter than anything Chapter 6 worked with.
- **Chapter 7** (`CH7-01`, `CH7-06`): the habit of adding a new
  observation category to the personal baseline log and judging it by
  trend rather than a single reading — already established for body and
  muscle condition — is extended here to facial-expression and
  mouth-related signs (`CH9-06`). A student who cannot recall how Chapter
  7 folded body condition into the existing log cannot correctly answer
  this chapter's "how would I know" question, because the answer is:
  *the same way, folded into the same log.*

## Concrete knowledge map

### Cluster A — Why these signs are the easiest of all to miss

- **`CH9-05`** *(DIRECT)*: Animals — including cats — usually conceal
  discomfort, so clinical signs of pain and dental disease "can be very
  subtle" even when the underlying condition is severe and painful.
  - *Owner relevance:* Directly explains why "she's still eating and
    using the box normally" is not reassurance — it is exactly what
    concealed pain looks like from the outside.
  - *Misconception corrected:* "No dramatic symptoms" means "nothing
    wrong."
  - *Callback:* This is the same underlying trap as `CH6-02`'s
    normalisation drift, now sharper: here the signal itself is
    biologically designed to be missed, not just gradually normalised.

### Cluster B — The five facial signs of acute pain

- **`CH9-01`** *(DIRECT)*: Five observable facial "Action Units" are
  associated with acute pain in cats: (1) **ear position** — upright and
  forward normally, versus rotated or flattened outward; (2) **orbital
  tightening** — a normally open eye versus a squinted or partially
  closed one; (3) **muzzle tension** — a relaxed, round muzzle versus a
  tense muzzle with a more elliptical shape; (4) **whisker change** —
  loose, curved whiskers spread from the face versus straight whiskers
  moved forward and/or bunched together; (5) **head position** — held
  above the shoulder line normally, versus level with the shoulders,
  lower, or tilted down.
  - *Owner relevance:* Gives the owner five concrete, nameable things to
    actually look at, replacing a vague sense of "she seems a bit off."
  - *Misconception corrected:* That pain in cats only shows up as
    obvious vocalising, limping, or hiding.
- **`CH9-02`** *(DIRECT)*: These five Action Units are not a folk
  observation — they are an empirically validated pain-scoring
  instrument (the Feline Grimace Scale), tested against cats with
  naturally occurring acute painful conditions (chiefly abdominal pain
  and urinary obstruction) compared with pain-free cats, with good
  agreement between trained scorers and a strong correlation with an
  already-validated pain scale. It has **not** been validated for
  chronic or slow-onset pain (e.g. arthritis) — only for acute pain.
  - *Owner relevance:* Establishes that this is a genuinely tested tool,
    not folklore — while explicitly fencing off the one thing it does
    not cover (chronic pain, which Chapter 10 addresses separately).
  - *Scope note:* The specific validation statistics (inter-rater
    reliability, cutoff score, sensitivity/specificity) are clinician/
    researcher-facing detail; this chapter teaches their *existence* as
    evidence the tool is rigorous, not as numbers an owner needs to
    calculate at home (see `CH9-03`).

### Cluster C — What the owner's role actually is (and is not)

- **`CH9-03`** *(SYNTHESIS)*: An owner's role with these five Action Units
  is to **notice and describe** them to a vet — not to self-score a
  diagnostic cutoff and decide, alone, that Momo is or isn't in
  significant pain. The scale's own accuracy figures were established
  among trained observers, not owners; the study itself states that how
  reliable novice/lay scoring would be is still an open question.
  - *Owner relevance:* Prevents the chapter's most rigorous piece of
    evidence from being misused as a home diagnostic license — exactly
    the trap Cluster B's rigor could otherwise invite.
  - *Misconception corrected:* "There's a validated cutoff score, so I
    can just calculate it myself and decide what to do."
  - *Scope note:* Disclosed per `CLAUDE.md` §19 as a synthesis: no source
    states this owner/clinician boundary in exactly this form, but it
    follows directly from `S30`'s own trained-rater-only validation and
    this course's medical scope boundary (`CLAUDE.md` §21 point 8) —
    observe and describe, never diagnose or self-treat.

### Cluster D — The signs easy to miss in her mouth

- **`CH9-04`** *(DIRECT)*: Six owner-observable warning signs of dental
  disease: bad breath, excessive drooling, pawing at the face, difficulty
  eating, bleeding or recessed gums, and tooth loss.
  - *Owner relevance:* Gives a concrete checklist for a body part owners
    rarely inspect closely and cats actively resist having examined.
  - *Misconception corrected:* That dental problems would be obvious
    (visible broken teeth, refusing food outright) rather than subtle.
- **`CH9-05` (continued)** *(DIRECT)*: A definitive dental diagnosis
  requires general anesthesia, with dental probing/charting and
  full-mouth x-rays as the standard of care — meaning an owner-observed
  warning sign is a prompt to seek veterinary assessment, not something
  an owner can fully evaluate alone at home.
  - *Owner relevance:* Sets the correct ceiling on what owner-level
    observation can achieve here, consistent with this course's
    observe-record-escalate scope boundary.

### Cluster E — Reapplying Chapter 6's method to signs this quiet

- **`CH9-06`** *(SYNTHESIS)*: Facial-expression signs and mouth-related
  signs should be added to the same personal observation-log baseline
  already extended once for body/muscle condition (`CH7-06`) — checked
  during the same regular look-over, not as a separate new ritual to
  remember.
  - *Owner relevance:* Keeps the growing observation habit sustainable
    rather than turning into an ever-longer checklist nobody actually
    follows.
  - *Callback:* Directly extends `CH1-09`'s original daily-log habit and
    `CH7-06`'s body-condition addition to it.
- **`CH9-07`** *(SYNTHESIS)*: Chapter 6's four-step method applies
  directly to pain and dental signs: (1) recall baseline (how she
  normally eats, grooms, and holds her face/ears); (2) identify what
  changed (a new Action Unit present, a new dental sign, a grooming or
  eating change); (3) check the urgent-escalation signature (e.g. a
  complete refusal to eat, or a presentation matching `CH3-06`'s
  obstruction-risk signature); (4) if not urgent, check for a benign
  explanation (a single Action Unit appearing briefly during an
  inherently uncomfortable moment — a nail trim, being startled — is not
  the same as a sign persisting outside that context). Because these
  signs are individually quiet and ambiguous, `CH4-04`'s combined-signal
  principle matters more here than almost anywhere else in the course: no
  single Action Unit or single dental sign, alone and momentary, is
  conclusive — a *combination* of signs, or any one sign *persisting
  against baseline with no benign explanation*, is what should prompt
  raising it with the vet promptly rather than waiting for a scheduled
  visit.
  - *Owner relevance:* This is the chapter's actual answer to "how would
    I know" — not a new method, but the existing method, pointed at
    quieter evidence.
  - *Misconception corrected:* Both of this chapter's opening
    misconceptions at once: under-recognition (no method applied at all,
    so subtle signs are missed) and over-escalation (the method's step 3
    misapplied to a single ambiguous sign with an identifiable benign
    cause).
  - *Callback:* Directly reuses `CH6-01`, `CH6-02`, and `CH4-04`.
  - *Scope note:* Disclosed per `CLAUDE.md` §19 — this integration of the
    baseline-comparison method with facial/dental signs and combined-
    signal reading is this course's own construction, not a claim made
    by any one source.

## Owner-facing application

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to look for facial
pain signs and dental warning signs as part of the same observation
routine already built up since Chapter 1, correctly distinguish a single,
context-explained, momentary sign from a persistent or combined pattern
with no benign explanation, and respond with the right tier of action —
log and keep watching, raise it with the vet promptly, or escalate
immediately — rather than either dismissing subtle signs or panicking
over an isolated one.

## Opening scenario

**What the student sees:** Over about ten days, a few small things
accumulate that no one has mentioned to each other: Momo eats slightly
slower and seems to favour one side of her mouth; a housemate notices her
breath smells a bit different than usual; in a couple of photos taken for
an unrelated reason, her ears look a little back and her muzzle looks
tense; she's still eating every meal and using the litter box completely
normally. One housemate says there's nothing to worry about — "she's
eating fine." Another, alarmed by the photos, wants to treat it as an
emergency right now.

**What is deliberately withheld:** Whether this is pain, a dental
problem, both, or something else entirely — the chapter does not resolve
to a single diagnosis, because the owner's job is to notice the pattern
and describe it, not to diagnose it themselves.

**Decision the student must make:** What should the household actually do
with these small, individually ambiguous observations?

## Practice/applied activity

**Sign-sorting practice.** The student is given four short observation-log
excerpts and must apply Chapter 6's four-step method to each, stating
which tier of response is correct (log-and-watch, raise with the vet
promptly, or escalate immediately) and which claim justifies it. Profiles
vary a pedagogically real constraint (per `CLAUDE.md` §18) — how much and
how often each household realistically observes their cat — not a token
detail:

1. **A single owner with an unpredictable work schedule, who mostly
   interacts with the cat in the evening.** The log shows slower,
   one-sided eating and slightly noticeable bad breath, both present for
   four consecutive evenings, with no obstruction-type signature and no
   identifiable benign cause. → No emergency signature is present, but
   the pattern has persisted for several days with no benign
   explanation — raise it with the vet promptly (`CH9-04`, `CH9-07`),
   rather than waiting for it to resolve on its own or for the next
   scheduled visit.
2. **Retired housemates who are home most of the day and see the cat
   constantly.** The log shows one squinting episode that began right
   after a loud vacuum cleaner startled the cat and fully resolved within
   the hour, with nothing else unusual before or since. → An identifiable
   benign explanation accounts for the entire pattern, and it resolved —
   log and keep watching; this is not a case for escalation (`CH9-07`,
   directly exercising the over-escalation failure mode from `CH6-02`).
3. **A family with young children, who check in on the cat throughout the
   day but not always systematically.** The log shows a week of flinching
   whenever the cat is picked up around the abdomen, reduced appetite,
   and more time spent hiding, with no benign explanation identified. →
   Multiple corroborating signs, no benign explanation, persisting for a
   week — raise it with the vet promptly; if appetite loss becomes
   complete or a straining/no-urination pattern appears, this immediately
   escalates to the urgent-signature tier already taught in `CH3-06`.
4. **A shared-care household — two housemates who alternate weeks working
   from home, so no single person is present every day.** Over twelve
   days, three separate log entries by different housemates each note
   only one thing in isolation — less enthusiastic play, then four days
   later mild pawing at the mouth, then noticeable bad breath — and no
   single housemate, going only on their own memory, would have noticed
   more than one sign. → Read together in the shared log, this is a
   persisting cluster of corroborating signs with no benign explanation —
   raise it with the vet promptly (`CH9-04`, `CH9-07`). This profile is
   chosen to demonstrate, not just assert, why `CH9-06` puts facial and
   dental signs into the *same shared log* rather than relying on any one
   person's memory: the pattern is only visible because the observations
   were recorded, not because any individual observer was especially
   attentive.

## Transfer scenario/Case Check (primary)

**Scenario.** Momo's logged baseline shows a hearty morning eater who
grooms herself thoroughly and greets housemates at the door. Over ten
days: she eats slightly slower and favours one side of her mouth; her
grooming has dropped off noticeably on one side, leaving a slightly
unkempt patch near her mouth; several recent photos, taken for an
unrelated reason, show her ears held a little back and her muzzle looking
tense; a housemate notices her breath smells different than usual. She is
still eating every meal and using the litter box completely normally.

| Option | Text |
|---|---|
| A | Since she's still eating every meal and using the litter box normally, nothing important has changed — keep observing without raising anything further |
| B | Compare the specific changes against her logged baseline: note the combination of slower one-sided eating, the grooming drop-off in the same area, the Action Units visible across several recent photos, and the breath change; since no benign explanation accounts for the whole pattern and it has persisted for over a week, describe these specific observations to the vet promptly rather than waiting for a future scheduled visit |
| C | The photos showing ears back and a tense muzzle are enough on their own to score her using the published pain scale and conclude she's in significant pain — proceed with pain relief before any vet visit |
| — | **Answer: B** |

**Reasoning:** A repeats this chapter's first opening misconception —
treating normal eating and litter box use as proof nothing is wrong,
when concealment is exactly what pain and dental disease are expected to
look like (`CH9-05`). C misapplies `CH9-02`'s validated scale exactly the
way `CH9-03` warns against: the scale's accuracy figures were established
among trained observers, not owners scoring photos at home, and this
course never has an owner initiate pain relief on their own — that is a
vet-led decision, not an owner one (`CLAUDE.md` §21 point 8). B is the
only option that reads the *combination* of signs against baseline
(`CH9-06`, `CH9-07`), finds no benign explanation for the pattern as a
whole, and responds at the correct tier: describing specific observations
to a vet promptly, not waiting, and not self-treating.

## Secondary application case

**Pedagogical variable:** where the primary case tests recognising a
combined, persistent pattern that *does* warrant raising with the vet,
this case tests recognising when a single, momentary, context-explained
sign does *not* — the only place in this chapter the over-escalation
failure mode (`CH6-02`) is genuinely exercised on its own, rather than
just named alongside under-recognition.

**Scenario.** During a routine at-home nail trim, a friend's cat squints
tightly and flattens her ears for a few seconds, then relaxes completely
as soon as the trim is finished and returns to normal behaviour for the
rest of the day. The friend, having just learned about the five facial
Action Units, is now convinced her cat is in significant pain and wants
to take her to the emergency vet immediately.

| Option | Text |
|---|---|
| A | Recommend an immediate emergency vet visit, since two of the five validated Action Units were clearly present |
| B | Recognise that the squinting and ear-flattening appeared only during an inherently uncomfortable, momentary procedure and resolved completely right afterward — an identifiable benign context, not a persistent change against baseline — so no escalation is needed; it's still worth mentioning if either sign starts appearing outside that context |
| C | Conclude that the Action Units aren't a reliable tool at all, since they showed up during something completely harmless |
| — | **Answer: B** |

**Reasoning:** A is the over-escalation failure mode `CH6-02` warns
against, applied to a single momentary sign with an obvious benign
explanation — the same mistake as Beat 3's rejected option B, now shown
happening to someone else's cat rather than Momo's. C over-corrects in
the opposite direction, discarding a validated tool because one
non-representative, context-explained instance didn't indicate pain —
missing that `CH9-07`'s method exists precisely to tell these two
situations apart by context and persistence, not to treat every
appearance of an Action Unit as equally meaningful. B is the only option
that reads the sign in its actual context, matching how `CH9-07`'s
method resolves an isolated, explained, resolved sign as log-and-watch,
not escalate.

## Common misconceptions

1. If she's still eating and using the litter box normally, nothing is
   wrong.
2. Pain always shows up as something dramatic — vocalising, limping,
   hiding — not something as subtle as a facial expression.
3. Because a validated scoring scale exists, an owner can calculate the
   score themselves and decide, alone, whether she's in significant pain.
4. Dental problems would be obvious (a visibly broken tooth, refusing
   food outright) rather than subtle.
5. Any appearance of a "pain sign" — even a single, brief, context-
   explained one — should be treated as an emergency.
6. If one example of a "validated sign" turns out to be a false alarm,
   the whole tool must not be worth learning.

## Placement justification

**Why here:** This chapter depends on Chapter 6's method and Chapter 7's
baseline-extension habit, and neither depends on Chapter 8 — its position
as Part III's final chapter reflects the human decision gate's approval
of it as a capstone-style reasoning chapter, deliberately following the
Part's factual chapters (7, 8) so the student has already practised
extending the observation log twice before being asked to extend it to
its quietest category yet.

**Progression-swap test:** If this chapter were moved earlier in Part
III, the student would not yet have Chapter 6's method (a hard
dependency) or Chapter 7's baseline-extension habit (directly reused in
`CH9-06`) established. If moved into Part IV, it would sit alongside
Chapter 10's chronic/aging-pain content despite deliberately teaching only
*acute* pain recognition for a still-young Momo — collapsing a
distinction this course's evidence base (`docs/SOURCES.md`, `S30`'s
acute-only validation) deliberately preserves.

**What is lost if removed:** Without this chapter, Part III would teach
environment, feeding, preventive care, and lifestyle design, but leave the
student with no method for the single hardest category of problem in
cat care — one the cat herself is built to conceal — right before Part IV
asks the student to catch exactly this kind of change over a period of
years, not days.
