## Teaching purpose

Addresses three linked misconceptions:

1. That a hiding, withdrawn kitten on arrival day is a problem to be fixed
   through reassurance, coaxing, or handling — rather than a normal,
   protective coping behaviour that calls for the *owner* to change their
   own behaviour (reduce demands, provide safe resources, wait).
2. That "getting ready for a cat" means buying supplies, not
   hazard-proofing the home and lining up a vet visit — so owners are
   caught off guard by risks (a houseplant, a dropped pill, a resident
   pet's health status) that were entirely foreseeable.
3. That "settling in" is a thing that happens *to* the owner (something to
   watch and wait out) rather than something the owner should be actively
   recording from day one — because without a personal baseline for this
   specific cat, later chapters (and later parts of the course) have
   nothing to compare against.

## Concrete knowledge map

Grouped by cluster, each with its meaning, why it matters to an owner, and
the misconception it corrects. Claim IDs reference `docs/SOURCES.md`.

### Cluster A — Before she arrives: hazard-proofing the home

- **True lilies are lethal, not just "mildly toxic."** *Lilium* and
  *Hemerocallis* species (Easter, Tiger, Asian, Stargazer, Day lilies, etc.)
  can cause life-threatening kidney injury from leaves, stems, petals, or
  even pollen — remove them entirely rather than "keep them out of reach"
  (`CH1-07`). *Owner relevance:* this is the single highest-consequence,
  lowest-effort thing an owner can get wrong before day one. *Misconception
  corrected:* "toxic plant" sounds like a mild stomach upset; for true
  lilies specifically, it means a medical emergency from minimal exposure.
- **Ordinary human medication is a real risk.** Common pain relievers
  (ibuprofen, naproxen) should be stored where a curious kitten cannot
  knock them loose (`CH1-07`). *Owner relevance:* these are already in most
  homes, unlike an exotic hazard. *Misconception corrected:* hazard-proofing
  is not just about buying cat-specific dangerous items and avoiding them —
  ordinary household items the owner already owns are the more likely risk.
- **Flea/tick and insecticide products must be cat-labelled.** A product
  safe for dogs or the yard is not automatically safe for a cat (`CH1-07`).
  *Misconception corrected:* "pest control" is treated as a single category
  of product, when species-specific formulation is the actual safety
  boundary.

### Cluster B — The arrival window: coping, not misbehaving

- **Hiding after arrival is a coping behaviour**, not a sign something is
  wrong with the kitten or the adoption (`CH1-01`). *Owner relevance:*
  determines whether the owner's first instinct (retrieve/reassure) helps
  or harms. *Misconception corrected:* stillness and avoidance read as
  "something's wrong," when they are the expected response to a stressful
  new environment.
- **A "safe place" is a small, enclosed, defensible space** — under
  furniture, inside a covered carrier or box — that reduces stress even
  without full physical concealment from a real threat (`CH1-02`).
- **Essential resources should be arranged inside a small initial
  territory**, with litter physically separated from food and water
  (`CH1-03`).
- **Access to the rest of the home should expand gradually**, not all at
  once (`CH1-06`, `SYNTHESIS`).
- **The carrier should stay out as a familiar object**, not appear only
  before vet visits (`CH1-04`).
- **Interaction is initiated by the kitten, not the human**: no picking up,
  chasing, or forced petting in the first 72 hours (`CH1-05`).

### Cluster C — After she arrives: the veterinary screening step

- **A newly adopted cat of unknown health history should see a vet as soon
  as possible**, including FeLV/FIV screening and a parasite check, and
  should be kept separate from any resident cats until that screening is
  done (`CH1-08`). *Owner relevance:* this is a concrete action item with a
  deadline (days, not "eventually"), and it directly protects any existing
  pets. *Misconception corrected:* "she seems healthy and settled" is not
  the same evidence as a clean bill of health — some risks (FeLV/FIV,
  internal parasites) are invisible to a new owner's observation.

### Cluster D — Starting the habit the rest of the course depends on

- **A simple daily observation log starts on day one**: roughly how much
  she ate and drank, whether the litter box was used and how, her energy
  level, and whether she was hiding or exploring (`CH1-09`, `SYNTHESIS`).
  *Owner relevance:* this is the single most load-bearing new idea in this
  revision — Chapter 3 and every later "is this normal for her?" judgement
  in the course depends on the student already having a personal baseline,
  not a generic one. *Misconception corrected:* "settling in" is not a
  passive waiting period for the owner; it is the first active data-
  collection the owner ever does about this specific cat.

## Owner-facing application

By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to walk into their own
home before a cat arrives and identify what to remove or secure; set up one
room as a low-pressure arrival space with correctly separated resources;
resist the urge to coax a hiding kitten out; book and prepare for a
post-arrival vet visit; and start a one-line-a-day log of eating, drinking,
litter use, and energy level that they can already imagine using in Chapter
3.

## Opening scenario

**Status: PROPOSED (draft wording; structure/topic is approved).**

Momo, a ~10-week-old kitten, has just arrived at her new home. Within
minutes of being let out of the carrier, she bolts and disappears under the
bed in the spare room. The student (in the role of the new owner) can hear
her but not see her.

What the student sees: an empty room, a visibly used but empty carrier, and
no visible cat.

What is deliberately withheld: whether this is "normal," whether Momo is
frightened specifically of this house or of the move itself, and how long
this might last — the withholding is deliberate, so the student must decide
without that reassurance, the way a real new owner would.

Decision the student must make: what to do right now, in the first few
minutes.

*(`PROPOSED` addition: a short beat, before this scenario, establishes that
the room was already hazard-proofed and the carrier is the same one Momo
travelled in — so the "prepare" cluster is dramatized, not just told.)*

## Opening question

**Status: PROPOSED (draft wording; structure is approved — three
non-strawman options, per the original teaching-format brief).**

**Question:** Momo won't come out from under the bed. What should you do?

| Option | Text | Intended? | Why tempting | Misconception revealed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Reach under the bed and gently pull her out so she can start getting used to the house | No | Feels caring and proactive — "the sooner she meets everyone/everything, the sooner she'll settle" | Treats forced exposure as bonding, when it removes the kitten's sense of control and increases stress |
| B | Leave her food, water, a litter box and a hiding spot nearby, and let her come out when she's ready | **Yes** | — | — |
| C | Leave her completely alone in a closed room with no food or litter box nearby, and check on her in a day or two | No | Sounds like "giving her space," which is partly right in spirit | Confuses *reducing pressure* with *neglecting resources* — space without resources is not what "safe place" guidance means |

## Practice / applied activity

**Status: PROPOSED (description only — no UI/interaction implementation at
this stage).** The student is given a small floor-plan of an unfamiliar
home (not their own, to keep it a transferable skill rather than a personal
task) and asked to: (1) flag at least three plausible hazards from a mixed
set of hazardous and safe household objects; (2) place the five required
resources (food, water, litter, rest, hide) respecting the litter-separation
rule; (3) write one realistic log entry for "day one" using the format
introduced in beat 10. This is a single applied exercise doing double duty
for clusters A, B, and D — not three separate activities — to avoid padding.

## Transfer scenario / Case Check

**Status: PROPOSED draft.**

**Scenario:** It's the first evening. Several friends are excited to meet
Momo and want to come over to see her and say hello.

**Options:**
- A. Invite them over — Momo will have to get used to visitors eventually, so it may as well start now.
- B. Ask them to wait a few days, and when they do visit, keep the visit calm, let Momo choose whether to appear, and don't let anyone pick her up.
- C. Cancel all future visits indefinitely until Momo is fully settled.

**Answer:** B.

**Reasoning:** This is not a repeat of the opening question (which was about
the first few minutes) — it tests whether the student can apply "small
territory, no forced interaction, gradual exposure" to a *new* decision (a
social/visitor situation). A rewards impatience over the kitten's pace; C
over-corrects into treating "give space" as "give total isolation
forever" — the same misconception as wrong option C in the opening question,
at a later point in time.

**Misconception tested:** That settling in is either "get it over with fast"
(A) or "avoid everything indefinitely" (C), rather than a gradually managed
process with clear, low-stress rules (B).

## Common misconceptions

Consolidated from the clusters above (see each cluster for detail and
sourcing):

1. A hiding kitten needs to be coaxed or retrieved, not left to cope on its
   own terms (`CH1-01`).
2. "Toxic plant" and other household hazards are a minor, generic risk
   category rather than specific, foreseeable, high-consequence items
   (`CH1-07`).
3. A kitten that looks healthy and calm has been medically cleared —
   invisible risks (FeLV/FIV, parasites) require an actual vet visit
   (`CH1-08`).
4. "Giving space" means passive waiting, not the owner's first active data
   collection about this specific cat (`CH1-09`).

## Placement justification

**Why here:** this chapter is the only place in the course where "a cat has
just arrived and knows nothing about this home" is true — hazard-proofing,
first-72-hours behaviour, and starting a fresh observation log are all
specific to that one-time state. **What breaks if moved:** every later
chapter assumes the observation habit (`CH1-09`) already exists; moving
this chapter later would leave Chapter 3 with no established baseline to
reason from. **What is lost if removed:** without it, the course would
either skip straight to "reading" an already-settled cat (undermining the
course's own "instruction-following owner → observer" progression in
`docs/CURRICULUM.md`) or would have to smuggle arrival content into a later
chapter not built to carry it.
