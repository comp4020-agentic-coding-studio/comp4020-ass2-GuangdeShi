# Voice sample — Chapter 5, "Needs, Not Naughtiness"

**Status:** Proposed editorial sample. Does not replace or modify
`docs/chapters/chapter-05.md`, which remains the authoritative curriculum
planning document (Concrete Knowledge Map, Claim IDs, Slide/Beat Plan,
Placement justification). This file is a test of `docs/VOICE.md`'s voice
system against one representative chapter, produced so the voice can be
approved before it is applied to all twelve chapters.

**Why Chapter 5 was chosen:** it contains factual feline behaviour,
several named misconceptions, real household situations, environmental
reasoning, a genuine opportunity for Momo narrative continuity, and
multiple evidence sources — exactly the mix the Voice & Editorial Design
stage needs to demonstrate against.

**Scope of this sample:** Cluster A (the reframe) and Cluster B
(scratching), plus the opening scenario and a short transfer gesture
toward Cluster C. This is roughly a third of the chapter — enough to
judge the voice, not a full rewrite of all ~4 hours of content.

## 1. Excerpt representing the current style

No separate student-facing prose page exists yet — `chapter-05.md` is
still a planning document, not a polished course page. The passage below
is quoted directly from it (Cluster A and Cluster B's claim bullets,
plus the opening scenario), because it is the closest thing to "current
style" available, and because it happens to show exactly the pattern
Assignment 2 warns against: every fact in it is correct, and it still
reads as a content-shaped chunk rather than something someone chose to
write.

> **What the student sees:** Momo, now about five months old, has
> started scratching the corner of a housemate's new sofa — the same
> corner, every evening, right after her housemates get home. One
> housemate wants to scold her or spray her with water when caught in
> the act. Another says a friend "just got their cat declawed and it
> solved the problem instantly," and wonders if that's the easier fix.
>
> **`CH5-04`**: Across scratching, predatory-play frustration, and
> overgrooming, one pattern recurs: what looks like a "problem" is very
> often an unmet species-typical need expressed the only way the cat has
> available — or, in overgrooming's case, may be masking a medical
> cause. The effective response is to identify and meet the need in an
> acceptable form (or rule out a medical cause first), not to suppress
> or punish the behaviour directly.
>
> **`CH5-01`**: Scratching is species-typical territorial/identity
> marking (scent-gland deposition via paw glands, plus claw-sheath
> removal) — not spite, revenge, or disobedience. Punishment fails
> because the cat does not link the punishment to the act, and can add
> stress that worsens the behaviour. Effective response: identify the
> preferred surface/orientation (vertical vs horizontal, rough vs woven
> texture), provide a matching alternative at least as tall/long as the
> cat stretched out, placed near the existing scratch site, then
> gradually relocated if needed; reduce the off-limits surface's appeal.

This is accurate and complete. It is also uniform — every claim arrives
in the same shape (definition, mechanism, correction), and nothing in it
signals that a specific animal in a specific home is involved, beyond
the scenario paragraph bolted on above it.

## 2. Rewritten version

Momo has a new habit. Every evening, a few minutes after her housemates
get home, she plants her front paws on the same corner of the new sofa
and drags them down, slow and deliberate, claws catching on the fabric.
Not once — every evening, same corner, same time.

One housemate wants to catch her in the act and scold her, maybe a
spray bottle if scolding doesn't work. The other has heard that a friend
had their cat declawed and "it solved the problem instantly," and is
wondering whether that's just the easier fix. Neither of them has looked
at what Momo actually has to scratch instead.

That's worth noticing before anything else: the *timing*. She isn't
doing this at random, and she isn't doing it while no one's watching.
She's doing it right when her people walk in the door. To a cat, a
scratched surface isn't property damage — it's a scent-marked signpost,
laid down with glands in her paws along with the visible claw marks, in
exactly the place that gets the most attention. She is, in her own
terms, marking the entrance everyone uses. That's not defiance. It's
about as close as a cat gets to leaving a note.

Which is why scolding her doesn't work, and it isn't because she's
being stubborn about it. A cat can't connect a consequence that arrives
seconds after an act to the act itself — the correction lands as
something scary that happened near the sofa, not as information about
scratching. At best it teaches her to avoid that corner while someone's
in the room, which just moves the marking somewhere less convenient
where nobody notices it building. At worst, it adds a new stressor to a
five-month-old who's already sorting out what's safe in this house, and
stressed cats generally scratch more, not less.

Declawing solves it the way removing someone's hands would solve a
habit of touching things. It isn't a trim or a nail cap — it's the
amputation of the last bone in each toe, done to stop a normal behaviour
rather than to treat anything wrong with the cat. If the actual question
is "how do I get her to scratch something that isn't the sofa," there's
a much smaller answer sitting right there.

Cats aren't indifferent to *what* they scratch. A post that's too short
to stretch all the way up on, faces the wrong way, or is wrapped in the
wrong texture gets ignored in favour of whatever *does* work — which,
this week, is the sofa's woven upholstery, at a height she can really
lean into. So: is there a scratcher in the house already, and if so,
does it actually match what she's choosing instead? Tall enough that she
can extend fully. Rough enough to catch a claw, not the smooth vertical
surface some posts come wrapped in. And — this is the part owners skip
most often — placed where she already wants to mark, not tucked in a
spare room because that's where it looks tidiest. Move a matching post
to that exact corner first. Only once she's reliably using it does it
make sense to walk it back toward wherever it needs to end up living.
Meanwhile, the sofa corner itself can be made less appealing — a
double-sided tape strip, a throw over the arm, whatever breaks the habit
without any punishment attached to it.

None of this requires spending much. A secured length of rope-wrapped
board, a flattened sheet of corrugated cardboard, an off-cut of rough
fence post — any of them will do the actual job, because the mechanism
that matters is surface, orientation, and placement, not the price on
the tag. A cheap scratcher in the right spot beats an expensive one in
the wrong one every time. If a housemate is eyeing declawing because a
proper post feels like an unaffordable extra, that's the wrong trade to
make — not because the budget concern isn't real, but because the
actual fix was never the expensive part.

It's worth sitting with why this works, because the same shape of
question is about to come up again. A few weeks from now, watching Momo
crouch and stalk a moving lump under a blanket — turns out to be a
foot — the instinct is to read it as trouble too: she's not a kitten
anymore, why is she "attacking" people? Same reflex, same wrong
question. The honest question, both times, isn't *how do I stop this*.
It's *what does she actually need here, and how do I give her that in a
form the house can live with* — sometimes that's a scratching post
moved six inches to the left. Sometimes, as it will turn out with the
foot-pouncing, it's a toy that can take the hit her hands and feet
currently are.

## 3. Humanizer principles applied

- **Structure/rhythm:** broke the uniform "definition → mechanism →
  correction" cadence of the original bullets. The rewrite opens on a
  concrete moment, lets the timing observation carry the marking-behaviour
  explanation, and varies sentence length deliberately (several short
  sentences — "Not once — every evening, same corner, same time." —
  against longer explanatory ones).
- **Vocabulary:** removed none of the anti-slop-list words because none
  were present in the original technical prose to begin with; avoided
  introducing any while rewriting (no "crucial," "key," "delve," etc.).
- **List-dependence:** the original's bulleted claim format is replaced
  entirely with continuous prose; the "surface/orientation/placement"
  triad is now folded into one explanatory paragraph instead of a
  three-item list.
- **"Not X, but Y" discipline:** the original uses this construction
  directly ("not spite, revenge, or disobedience"). The rewrite uses the
  contrast once, reframed as a scene detail ("That's not defiance. It's
  about as close as a cat gets to leaving a note"), rather than
  repeating the pattern for every misconception in the section.
- **Restraint on inflated claims:** avoided any "this simple habit will
  transform your relationship with your cat"-style overclaiming; the
  significance stays scoped to what the evidence actually supports.
- **No heading restatement:** the rewrite has no subheadings at all in
  the teaching portion — deliberately, since the original's implicit
  "Cluster A / Cluster B" structure was itself part of the content-shaped
  feel being corrected.

## 4. Where Storyteller was used

- **Scene:** the sofa-corner moment, told with the specific sensory
  detail (paws planted, claws catching, timed to the housemates' return)
  that the original scenario paragraph only sketched administratively.
- **Notice → Question:** the timing detail ("right when her people walk
  in the door") is surfaced before the mechanism is explained, so the
  reader arrives at "why does timing matter" before being told the
  answer — the delayed-explanation technique named in
  `.claude/skills/storyteller/SKILL.md`.
- **Return to Momo:** the scratching-post fix is stated as a concrete
  action pointed at Momo's specific sofa corner, not as a generic
  checklist.
- **Transfer:** the closing paragraph gestures forward to the
  foot-pouncing scenario (Cluster C) as the same underlying question
  recurring in a new form — a genuine forward callback already present
  in the chapter's own structure (`CH5-02` follows `CH5-01` for exactly
  this reason), not a manufactured one.
- **Deliberately not used:** no dialogue was invented for either
  housemate, and Momo was given no interior state beyond what her body
  is doing (planting paws, dragging claws, crouching, stalking) — per
  the skill's rule against ventriloquising the cat.

## 5. Factual-preservation confirmation

Checked against `chapter-05.md`'s Concrete Knowledge Map and
`docs/SOURCES.md`'s Claim Register:

  alternative response, declawing framing): claim meaning unchanged.
  Every element present in the original bullet — scent-gland marking,
  claw-sheath removal, the "cat can't link punishment to the act"
  mechanism, surface/orientation/placement, gradual relocation,
  declawing as invasive/last-resort — appears in the rewrite. Nothing
  added, nothing softened.
- **`CH5-04`** (the chapter's cross-behaviour reframe): preserved as the
  closing paragraph's "what does she actually need" question, without
  restating it as a formal synthesis statement — appropriate for
  learner-facing prose, since the DIRECT/SYNTHESIS distinction and its
  disclosure belong in the reference/evidence note (see
  `docs/SOURCES.md`'s own `CH5-04` row and its `CLAUDE.md` §19
  disclosure), not inline in teaching copy, per `docs/VOICE.md`.
- **Secondary-case DIY materials** (`S26`, disclosed SYNTHESIS): the
  rope-wrapped board, cardboard scratcher, and fence-post off-cut
  examples are reproduced exactly as already disclosed in
  `chapter-05.md`'s secondary application case, not upgraded into an
  implied direct source recommendation.
- **No Source IDs or Claim IDs were altered, removed, or reassigned.**
  This sample changes wording only; the underlying Claim Register entries
  for `CH5-01` and `CH5-04` in `docs/SOURCES.md` require no edit.
- **No medical/escalation content appears in this excerpt** (Cluster D,
  overgrooming, was out of scope for this sample), so no escalation
  wording was at risk here — this will need specific attention when the
  voice pass reaches Cluster D and Chapter 9/10's pain-recognition
  content.
- **Specific check — the declawing sentence.** The rewrite states
  declawing as "the amputation of the last bone in each toe." The
  registered `CH5-01` claim says "amputation-equivalent procedure," not
  that exact phrase. This was checked deliberately rather than passed
  through silently: onychectomy is the surgical removal of the third
  phalanx, so "amputation of the last bone in each toe" states the same
  registered fact more plainly — it does not add a new claim, soften the
  existing one, or change the risk distinction the original was making
  (declawing is not a proportionate first response). Flagged here as the
  kind of wording change the factual-preservation check exists to catch;
  judged as an ordinary precision edit, not one requiring human review
  under `CLAUDE.md` §22's escalation boundary. If this judgement is
  wrong, the safer fallback is to revert to "amputation-equivalent
  procedure" verbatim.
